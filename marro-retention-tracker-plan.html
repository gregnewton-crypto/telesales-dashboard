The Retention Tracker

A build plan for the living version of the retention work — the page you open on a Monday to see how many households paused yesterday, and on which day of their life with us. Workstream of the Retention Improvement Master Plan.

Owner: Data & Retention Strategy Surface: GitHub Pages, extending the existing dashboard Source of truth: Airtable Blocking dependency: a pause event log
Pauses per day needs a pause date Marro has none that is live Day 5 after delivery is the cliff Build the log before the charts

The Short Answer

Two questions were asked: where should this dashboard live, and how do we track pauses per day. The second one turns out to be the harder of the two, and it is not a charting problem.

Where it lives GitHub Pages, as a new tab on the dashboard that already exists at index.html. Not a Google Sheet. The site is already wired to the Airtable API, already has a Retention tab, and already has a Daily tab carrying the placeholder “coming in v2”. This plan is that v2.
What the data is Airtable stays the source of truth. Google Sheets earns exactly one role — a landing strip for the daily Looker or Snowflake export — and only if we cannot write to Airtable directly. It should never be the thing anyone opens.
What blocks it Nothing in Marro’s live data records when a subscription paused. We have pause reasons on 26,699 subscriptions and pause dates on none of them that are current. Until that is fixed, pauses per day cannot be counted at all, on any surface.
What to build first A Marro Pause Events table: one row per pause, with a date, both clocks, and a reason. It is roughly a day of pipeline work and it unlocks every chart on this page. Build the log before the dashboard.
The one-sentence version We do not have a dashboard problem, we have a missing-column problem: add a pause date to the Marro feed, and the tracker becomes a weekend of front-end work on a page that is already built.

Why Pauses Per Day Is Currently Impossible

This is the finding that shapes the whole plan, so it is worth being precise about it. Marro’s subscription table stores current state, not history.

A row tells you where someone ended up

Each subscription carries five mutually exclusive flags — retained, unretained, paused, scheduled, undelivered — plus a First Pause Reason and a Pause Reason. Read a row and you learn the outcome.

It never tells you when

There is no pause date, no status-change timestamp, and no history table. The Last Modified field moves whenever anything on the row is edited, so it cannot stand in for one.

So the question has no answer

“How many people paused on Tuesday?” requires a pause date on every pause. No chart, spreadsheet formula or clever roll-up can recover a date that was never written down.

A second trap: a pause reason is not a churn

7,813 subscriptions carry a First Pause Reason and are marked retained — that is 67% of all retained customers. People pause and come back, or pause and get saved. Any chart that counts rows-with-a-pause-reason as churn will overstate the problem by a factor of roughly two, which is exactly the sort of error that gets a dashboard quietly distrusted and then ignored.

What this means for the brief The ask was a dynamic pause tracker. The honest answer is that the dynamism is not the hard part — the dashboard already refreshes itself every five minutes. The hard part is that the underlying event has never been recorded. Fix the feed and the tracker is straightforward. Skip the feed and no amount of front-end work will produce the chart.

Pauses Per Day Is Three Charts, Not One

“Pauses per day” means three genuinely different things, and they answer different questions. The tracker should carry all three, clearly labelled, because conflating them is how a programme ends up firing its most important message on the wrong day. The First 72 Hours page already calls this out as two clocks; the data below settles which clock the famous day-5 cliff is actually on.

1. The calendar Pauses that happened yesterday, regardless of who they were. This is the operational number — the one that tells you Tuesday was bad and someone should look into it. This is what most people mean by “pauses per day”.
2. The order clock Days between checkout and pause. Day 0 is the sale. This is the clock the sales floor feels, and it is the right one for judging an agent’s cohort.
3. The bowl clock Days between the first box landing and the pause. Day 0 is the first meal. This is the clock the cat is on, and it is the one the CRM has to trigger against.

The day-5 cliff is real, and it is on the bowl clock On the order clock day 5 is unremarkable — 473 pauses, well below day 7. Measured from the first delivery it is the tallest bar in the dataset at 915, nearly double its neighbours. Since a Marro box typically lands two days after checkout, the same event appears as day 7 on one clock and day 5 on the other. That is the whole discrepancy. It also confirms the operational instruction on the First 72 Hours page: every message must fire off delivery confirmation, not order date, or the day-3 check-in lands while the box is still on a van.

Why the reason mix makes the same argument

Splitting those pauses by reason shows two distinct churn engines rather than one, which is the thesis the master plan is built on. Before the box arrives, nobody complains about the food, because there is no food — they are cancelling a subscription. Once the bowl is down, refusal dominates. Later, price takes over.

Reconciling The Headline Numbers

Two figures quoted throughout the master plan do not reproduce from the Airtable data. This is not a reason to distrust the strategy — the shape of the problem is confirmed emphatically — but the tracker will publish these numbers continuously, so they need to agree with the decks before it launches.

Claim in the master plan Quoted From Airtable Most likely explanation
Pauses inside 7 days 56.67% 28.26% Measured as days 0–6 on the order clock. But days 0–13 on the order clock gives 57.27%, which is within rounding of the quoted figure. The original is almost certainly a two-week window — ISO weeks 1 and 2 — described as “7 days”.
Pause before any box arrives ~23% 4.94% Only 430 of 8,698 dated pauses precede the first delivery date. Coincidentally, 23.78% of pauses fall inside 7 days of signup on matured cohorts, so the two figures may have been transposed at some point. Needs settling at source.
Day 5 is the cliff — Confirmed Correct, on the bowl clock, at 915 pauses. The master plan attributes it to the order clock, where the peak is actually day 7. Worth correcting in the copy.
Gap to the 47% target 6.63/wk ~10.4/wk Current matured retention is 44.26% against a 47% target, on about 380 sales a week. The 6.63 figure came from a narrower matured window; both are defensible, but the tracker should compute it live rather than hard-code either.
Read this the right way Every one of these gaps is a definitional difference, not a contradiction. The strategy stands: early pauses dominate, refusal drives them, price takes over later. What is missing is a single written definition of each metric that the deck, the dashboard and the Looker export all share. That definition list is a deliverable of phase 1, not an afterthought.

The Missing Table

Owner: Data. Everything else on this page depends on it.

One new Airtable table in Telesales System, one row per pause event, appended daily and never overwritten. Small, boring, and the single highest-leverage thing in this plan.

How it gets populated — three options, in order of preference

A. Restore the Looker feed

⚙️ Marro D2MS Looker already has a First Pause Date column and already knows how to fill it. It simply stopped on 2 Feb 2026. Find out why, restart it, and schedule it daily.

Best because it is a repair, not a build, and it backfills history the moment it runs.

B. A Marro Snowflake table

Snowflake Butternut Sales exists and carries CURRENT_SUBSCRIPTION_STATUS. A Marro twin, snapshotted nightly, lets us derive pause events by watching status flip from active to paused.

Best because it survives the source system never emitting a pause timestamp at all.

C. Daily status snapshot

Failing both, a scheduled job copies the status of every live Marro subscription each night and diffs it against yesterday. Cruder, catches the date to within a day, and needs no cooperation from anyone upstream.

Worst because history starts the day we switch it on, so the first useful cohort view is a month away.

Whichever option wins, record every pause, not just the first First Pause Date is a single column on a subscription and therefore only ever tells us about pause number one. A household that pauses on day 5, gets saved, and pauses again on day 40 is two distinct events with two different stories, and the second one is invisible in the current model. The event log is what makes repeat pausing countable — and repeat pausing is the clearest possible signal that a save did not stick.

Three rules the build should not break

Never mix matured and immature cohorts A subscription signed up ten days ago cannot have failed to retain at 28 days. Include it in the denominator and retention will look like it is collapsing every time sales have a good week. Every retention figure states its maturity window on the panel.
Always show the denominator A 60% pause rate on 5 sales is noise. Panels carry their n, and anything below the agreed minimum cohort size renders greyed rather than ranked. This is also the anti-gaming guardrail the master plan asks for.
Label the clock on every chart Every day-based panel says which clock it is on, in the title, permanently. The cost of getting this wrong is a CRM message landing two days early for the whole base.

Technical notes for whoever builds it

  • Reuse the existing fetch layer in index.html — token in localStorage, paginated fetchAllRecords, the four-concurrent rate-limited queue. It already handles Airtable’s 5 requests-per-second ceiling.
  • Do not pull all 26,699 subscription rows into the browser on load. Fetch the event log, which is roughly a tenth of the size, and read totals from 📊 Weekly KPIs v2, which is already aggregated and already fetched.
  • Aggregate the daily series in an Airtable formula or a scheduled roll-up rather than in JavaScript. Browser-side grouping over tens of thousands of records is what makes dashboards feel broken on a phone.
  • A read-only Airtable token scoped to the two bases would remove the paste-your-token step. Worth checking whether that is acceptable for a public GitHub Pages URL before committing to it — if the repository is public, it is not, and the current bring-your-own-token approach is the right call.

Data Quality Fixes

Owner: Data. Cheap to fix now, expensive to explain later.

Found while reading the tables. None of these stop the build, but each one will produce a wrong number on a dashboard someone is about to trust.

ProblemWhereImpactFix
“Retention 28d” means the opposite of its name Marro sales It equals 1 − Retained on all 26,699 rows without exception. Anyone charting it as retention will report 55.7% when the truth is 44.3%, and the number is plausible enough that nobody will catch it. Rename to Not Retained 28d, or invert it. Highest priority on this table.
Pause reasons written into the postcode column Marro sales Rows carrying pet_did_not_eat_food as a postcode. A column shifted during an import. Find the affected import batch, re-run it, and add a postcode format check.
Broken VLOOKUPs frozen into a single-select Marro sales → Direct Sales Team Name Around 40 of the option values are literal #N/A (Did not find value 'Seb Wright' ...) strings. Every one is a real rep whose sales are attributed to a team that does not exist. Map each to its real team, merge the options, then fix the upstream lookup.
Timestamp is #ERROR! on every row Marro sales A dead column sitting next to a Timestamp MALFORMED column. Confusing and useless. Delete both, or repair one and delete the other.
Reason values are inconsistent free text Both pause reason fields Curly and straight apostrophes coexist, and one value ends in a stray underscore. Group-by will silently split the same reason into two rows. Convert to a single select with a fixed vocabulary; normalise on write.
Two pause-reason fields with no documented difference Marro sales First Pause Reason is populated on 21,413 rows and Pause Reason on 10,862, and it is not written down which is authoritative. Document it, or collapse both into the event log and retire them.
Reason taxonomy is too coarse to act on All sources pet_did_not_eat_food is the single largest reason at 8,509 rows, but it cannot distinguish texture, aroma, recipe or portion confusion — which are four different fixes owned by four different teams. Split at source. Until then, the offboarding call is the only place this detail is captured.

Where It Should Live

The question was Google Sheets or GitHub Pages. Here is the comparison honestly, including the case against the recommendation.

Option Strengths Why not Verdict
GitHub Pages
(extend index.html)		 Already built, already connected to Airtable, already refreshing every five minutes, already has the tab structure and the brand. Free, versioned, shareable by URL, and works on a phone. The Daily tab is literally a placeholder waiting for this. Needs someone who can write JavaScript to change it. Each viewer supplies their own Airtable token the first time. No row-level permissions. Recommended
Google Sheets Anyone can edit it. Pivot tables need no engineer. Good for a one-off analysis or a quick sanity check against the dashboard. Becomes a second source of truth within a fortnight. Manual refresh means it is stale exactly when someone quotes it in a meeting. Cohort survival and rolling averages over 26,699 rows are painful. Version history is not the same as a definition anyone agreed to. Landing strip only
Airtable Interface Zero pipeline — it sits on the data. Permissions, filtering and editing for free. Genuinely the fastest way to get the at-risk list in front of agents. Charting is limited: rolling averages and survival curves are awkward or impossible. Needs a seat per viewer. Cannot be shared with the agency as a link. Use for the at-risk list
Looker / BI tool Correct long-term home. Proper cohort analysis, governed metric definitions, scheduled delivery. Depends on data-team capacity and access we do not control. The Looker feeds we already depend on have been dead for six months, which is the argument against waiting for it. Later, if at all
The recommendation, and the reason behind it Build it on GitHub Pages as a new tab, because the alternative to extending something that already works is maintaining two things that half work. Keep Airtable as the only place a number is calculated, and let the page be a window onto it rather than a second opinion. Use Google Sheets for exactly one job — catching a daily export we cannot otherwise write into Airtable — and never send anyone a link to it.

Build Phases

Sequenced so that something useful ships before the hard dependency lands. Phase 0 needs nobody outside this team; phase 1 is where the pipeline conversation has to happen.

Phase 0 Agree the definitions
Write down what a pause is, which clock each metric uses, what counts as matured, and whether a reactivation cancels a pause. Settle the 56.67% and 23% discrepancies against the original review. One page, signed off by whoever owns the master plan. Nothing else here is safe to build until this exists.
Phase 1 Get a pause date flowing
Diagnose why the Marro Looker feed stopped on 2 February, restart it, and schedule it daily. If it cannot be revived, stand up the nightly status snapshot instead so history starts accumulating immediately while the better option is negotiated. This is the critical path and the only genuinely blocking item.
Phase 2 Build the event log
Create Marro Pause Events, backfill from the 8,784 dated pauses we already have, and add the derived clock and pre-delivery columns. At this point the analysis on this page becomes reproducible on demand rather than a one-off.
Phase 3 Ship the headline strip
Pauses per day with its rolling average, plus the days-to-pause histogram with the clock toggle. Replaces the “coming in v2” placeholder on the Daily tab. This is the thing that was asked for, and it should go live on its own rather than waiting for the rest.
Phase 4 Retention and reason panels
Box-2 retention against target with the live gap, reason mix over time, pre-delivery rate, and cohort survival curves. Runs in parallel with the data quality fixes, since those panels are the ones that expose bad data most visibly.
Phase 5 Agent view and the at-risk list
Per-agent cohorts with minimum-size guarding, and the daily at-risk queue surfaced in an Airtable interface where agents can work it. This is where the tracker stops reporting and starts changing the number.

Open Decisions

Answers needed before phase 2. Each one changes the schema or the panel set.

  • Which clock is the default? The bowl clock is the truer one and the day-5 cliff only appears on it, but the sales floor thinks in days since sale. Recommendation: bowl clock as default, order clock one toggle away, and never a chart without a label.
  • Is this Marro-wide or D2MS-only? The subscription table covers London, Manchester, Bristol, Scotland and Model Pitch as well as D2MS — D2MS is 7,358 of 26,699 rows. The dated Looker snapshot is D2MS-only, so a Marro-wide tracker needs a wider feed than the one we are restoring.
  • Does a reactivation erase a pause? Two-thirds of retained customers carry a pause reason, so this is not a corner case. Recommendation: keep the event, add a Reactivated flag, and let panels choose.
  • What is the minimum cohort size for agent panels? The master plan suggests 25 sales on a rolling four weeks. Worth confirming against the actual per-agent distribution before it drives any consequence.
  • Can the reason taxonomy be split at source? Everything downstream of pet_did_not_eat_food is guesswork until texture, aroma, recipe and portion confusion are separable. If the answer is no, the offboarding call script becomes the only sensor we have.
  • Who owns the feed when it breaks? It has broken once already, silently, for six months. A named owner and a staleness alert on the dashboard itself — “last updated” in red past 48 hours — matter more than any panel on this page.
The failure mode to design against The most likely way this project fails is not a bad chart. It is that the feed stops again, nobody notices for a month, and the numbers quietly drift until someone spots that the page disagrees with reality — at which point nobody trusts any of it again. Freshness monitoring is not a nice-to-have on this build. It is the feature that keeps the rest alive.
