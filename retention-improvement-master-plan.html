Executive Summary

Marro's retention problem has a name: the first bowl. Of every pause we recorded this year, 56.67% happened within seven days of sign-up, roughly 23% happened before a box was even delivered, and the single largest spike lands on day 5 — where the dominant reason given is that the cat did not eat the food. Our own blog content tells us why that timing matters: a kibble-fed cat needs 7–14 days to switch to fresh, and sometimes 3–4 weeks. Most churning customers are abandoning a fourteen-day process on day five, because nobody told them what day five is supposed to look like.

The data also tells us what is not wrong. Breed has no meaningful effect. Price barely registers in the first 28 days — it only becomes the dominant pause reason afterwards, which is exactly where our week-7 discount step-down bites. So we have two different churn engines and we have been treating them as one: days 0–28 is an acceptance problem solved with education, product experience and proactive support; day 28 onwards is a value problem solved with visible results and a smooth price taper instead of a cliff.

This plan reframes Box 1 from a pass/fail test into a Tasting & Texture Exploration Session. We capture what the cat was eating before sign-up, set a transition track relative to that baseline, redefine week-one success as "tasted and interested" rather than "cleaned the bowl", and intervene on day 3 — ahead of the day-5 cliff — instead of reacting to a pause we could have prevented. Work is organised into three pillars: Education, Messaging & Customer Experience; Agent Operations, QA & Enablement; and Data, Dashboards & Performance Management.

Strategic Thesis: Two Churn Engines, One Plan

The single most useful thing in the data is that the reason for churn changes at roughly the 28-day mark. If we run one retention playbook across the whole lifecycle we will spend margin where it is not needed and withhold support where it is.

  Engine 1 — Days 0 to 28 Engine 2 — Day 28 onwards
Dominant reason Pet did not eat the food; subscription friction and buyer's remorse before box 1. Price and perceived value, sharpening from week 7 as the discount steps down.
Peak risk Pre-delivery, then day 5, then days 7 and 14 as box 2 arrives. Week 7, then each renewal where spend visibly rises.
What works Expectation setting, transition coaching, aroma and texture tactics, proactive contact, free recipe swaps, feeding clarity. Proof of benefit (coat, digestion, energy, weight), plan right-sizing, loyalty pricing, taper instead of cliff.
What wastes money Discount-led saves. Price is not the objection yet, so a discount buys nothing and trains the customer to expect one. More education. They already know how to feed the cat; they are questioning the bill.
Owning pillar Pillar 1 and Pillar 2. Pillar 1 (initiative 10) and Pillar 3.
The reframe that everything hangs off Week one is not a verdict, it is a trial period for the cat's palate. If we define success on day 5 as "cleaned the bowl", most customers fail, and failure is the trigger for a pause. If we define success on day 5 as "sniffed it, licked it, ate some of the chicken", most customers pass — and passing is the trigger to keep going. Same cat, same bowl, different finish line.

Education, Messaging & Customer Experience

Owners: Retention Strategy, CRM, Brand & Content, Product. Goal: make the first fourteen days feel designed rather than survived.
Refined High impact Content + Retention

1. Transition Training & Content Positioning

Build a baseline-relative onboarding journey around food transition, using the blog material we already have. The critical upgrade to your original idea: the transition advice is worthless as a generic guide and powerful as a personalised one, because the length and difficulty of the switch depend almost entirely on what the cat ate before.

Strategy: anchor on the previous food

  • Capture "what is your cat eating today?" as a required field at sign-up (dry / wet / raw / mixed / brand where known), plus an eating-type question (easy, normal, fussy).
  • Assign one of four transition tracks from that answer — see Transition Tracks. A kibble cat gets a 14-day track and is told up front it may take 3–4 weeks. A raw-fed cat gets a 5-day track and is told to expect quick acceptance.
  • Every asset the customer receives states the day number and the expected state on that day, so nobody has to guess whether they are on track.

Framing: Box 1 is a Tasting & Texture Exploration Session

  • Rename Box 1 in all customer-facing copy. It is not "your first delivery", it is the tasting session — the week we find out which recipes and textures this particular cat loves.
  • Explicit instruction to try all flavours and textures rather than committing to one, and an explicit promise that we will rebuild Box 2 around the winners.
  • Lead with the myth-buster from the blog: kibble is coated in fat and flavour sprays after cooking, so a cat turning its nose up at fresh food is showing a habit, not a preference. This single line converts a customer's "my cat hates it" into "my cat is addicted to the old stuff", which is a solvable problem rather than a verdict.
  • Teach the three highest-leverage mechanics loudly and repeatedly: warm the food to lift aroma (warm water for 10 minutes or 10 seconds in the microwave), serve a spoonful on a clean plate with the old food and treats removed, and apply the 20-minute rule — if it is refused, take it away and offer the usual food, then try again tomorrow.
Deliverables
Four track guides, a printed Day 1–14 card for Box 1, four short videos (warming, plate setup, 20-minute rule, introducing recipe two), refreshed blog hub page.
Primary KPI
Day 0–7 pause rate; share of day-5 pauses citing food refusal.
Watch out
Do not promise instant success anywhere in the funnel. Sales copy that says "cats love it" is what creates the day-5 disappointment.
Refined High impact Fast to ship CRM

2. Onboarding CRM & Key-Day Interventions

Rebuild the email and SMS journey around the actual risk windows in the data rather than a generic weekly cadence. Three windows matter: pre-delivery (23% of pauses), day 3 to day 5 (the cliff), and days 7 and 14 (box 2 arriving). Full schedule in the Key-Day Intervention Map.

  • Pre-delivery block. The customer has paid and now has days of silence to reconsider. Fill it: delivery tracking, what is in the tasting box, how to prep, and an explicit "here is what week one looks like" expectation-setter. Confirm the subscription terms plainly here rather than letting box 2 be a surprise.
  • Day 3 pre-emption, not day 5 reaction. Message before the cliff, not after. Ask one question with a one-tap answer: ate it all / ate some / sniffed and walked away / refused. Each answer routes to a different next message and, for the worst two, a rescue action (see idea N5).
  • Texture-refusal branch. A dedicated flow for "refused" that leads with warming, plate hygiene, removing kibble between meals, and switching to the chicken recipe first because it is easiest on the stomach. Offer a free recipe swap before the customer thinks to ask for a pause.
  • Box 2 pre-notification. Two days before dispatch, tell them it is coming and give them a self-serve way to shift the date. Pausing is currently the only lever they have, so they use it.
  • Fussy-eater variant. Cats flagged fussy get a slower, more reassurance-heavy version of every message in the sequence.
Primary KPI
Day-3 check-in response rate, day 0–7 pause rate, pre-delivery pause rate, day 7 and 14 pause spikes.
Test design
Holdout of 20% on the existing journey for at least four full cohorts before rollout.
Refined Test first Medium impact Commercial + Retention

3. Six-Week Commitment Trial (2-Box Programme)

Test a two-box commitment with a deeper upfront discount in exchange for a longer commitment. The logic is sound — the data says customers who take longer to stick around stay longer — but the framing needs care and the measurement needs to be honest.

Refinements

  • Sell it as a programme, not a lock-in. "The 6-Week Transition Programme: the time it actually takes a cat to switch, at our best price." That is a benefit. "Commit to six weeks" is a hurdle, and roughly a quarter of our early pausers are already subscription-averse, so a hurdle will cost us conversions at checkout.
  • Do not count guaranteed weeks as retention. Holding someone for six weeks by contract is not retention, it is deferral. Judge this test only on week 7–12 and day-84 retention against a control. If the curve simply falls off a cliff at week 7, the trial has bought time, not loyalty.
  • Pair with flexibility inside the commitment. Free recipe swaps, portion changes and one date shift. The commitment is to the programme, not to a specific set of trays.
  • Mind the discount cliff. A deeper upfront discount makes the week-7 step-down steeper, and week 7 is already where retention breaks. Pair with initiative 10 so the exit is a taper.
  • Segment the test. Kibble-fed and fussy-flagged cats have the most to gain from a longer runway; those cells should show the biggest lift. Run the trial against 7-day and 14-day trial controls, since 14-day plans currently retain worse.
Primary KPI
Day-84 retention and contribution margin per acquired customer, not week-6 survival.
Guardrail
Checkout conversion and pre-delivery pause rate must not deteriorate.
New — gap found High impact Commercial

10. Week-7 Value Bridge & Discount Taper

None of the original nine initiatives owns the week-7 drop, and it is the clearest structural break in the whole dataset. Retention falls when the discount changes, which tells us we are currently renting customers rather than converting them to full price.

  • Replace the single step-down with a two- or three-step taper so no invoice jumps sharply in one week.
  • Land a "six weeks in" proof moment before the price change: the benefits the blog promises are visible by then (coat, digestion, energy, weight), so ask for a photo, show a before-and-after, and reflect their own logged transition progress back to them.
  • Offer plan right-sizing rather than a discount: a smaller portion band or a longer delivery interval keeps the customer at full unit price while cutting their weekly spend. Larger plans retain better, so protect plan size where the feeding maths supports it.
  • Introduce a loyalty price that is earned by staying, not a retention discount that is earned by threatening to leave.
Primary KPI
Week 6 to week 9 retention delta; share of post-28-day pauses citing price.

Agent Operations, QA & Enablement

Owners: Customer Service Lead, QA, Training. Goal: every agent conversation either prevents a food-refusal pause or recovers one.
Refined High impact Fast to ship CS Operations

4. Targeted Offboarding & Early-Pause Recovery Calls

Proactive outbound to customers who pause or cancel in their first days, to gather qualitative feedback and recover the account. Two refinements make this materially more valuable.

  • Call within 24 hours, and prioritise by reason. A "pet did not eat" pause on day 4 is highly recoverable because the transition was never finished. A price pause on day 60 needs a different conversation. Queue food-refusal pauses first.
  • Recover with product, not margin. Price is not the objection inside 28 days. The save offer should be a free recipe or texture swap, a rescue kit, a portion reset, or a delivery date shift — not a discount. Reserve discount authority for post-28-day price pauses.
  • Structured reason capture. A fixed taxonomy on every call: which recipe, which texture, how many days tried, was it warmed, was old food removed, how much was offered. This is how we learn which recipes and textures actually fail, which the pause dropdown will never tell us.
  • Feed it back weekly. The top three refusal patterns each week go straight to Content, CRM and Product. If one recipe or texture dominates refusals, that is a product decision, not a call-script decision.
Primary KPI
Save rate on early pauses, reactivation rate within 14 days, cost per save.
Refined Medium impact QA

5. Retention QA Scorecard

A call-scoring framework rating agents 1–5 on how effectively they guide customers through the transition. The refinement: score the specific behaviours the data says correlate with retention, not general call quality, and weight them.

Dimension (1–5) What a 5 looks like Weight
Baseline captured Previous food, brand and eating type recorded accurately in the record. 15%
Expectation set States the transition length for that baseline and that partial eating in week one is normal and expected. 20%
Mechanics taught Warming, clean plate, remove old food and treats, the 20-minute rule, start with chicken. 20%
Feeding clarity Confirms grams per day and how to split meals; customer can repeat the number back. 10%

  • Validate the scorecard against outcomes every quarter. If high scorers do not retain better, the scorecard is wrong — change it rather than defending it.
  • Build a clip library of real calls that scored 5 on expectation setting. Nothing teaches faster.
Refined Medium impact Training

6. Mandatory Retention E-Learning & Certification

A quarterly course and quiz that agents must pass to work on active retention campaigns. Refinement: make the curriculum the transition problem specifically, and make certification mean something operationally.

Curriculum, built from our own content

  • Module 1 — Fresh versus kibble, wet and raw: the real differences, in plain language.
  • Module 2 — Myth-busting: kibble and teeth, "my cat loves kibble", premium kibble, the 4% meat rule, raw safety.
  • Module 3 — The four transition tracks and what each day should look like.
  • Module 4 — The fussy-eater playbook: aroma, warming, texture ladder, plate hygiene, the 20-minute rule, patience scripts.
  • Module 5 — Feeding plans and grams per day: how to explain the number and split meals.
  • Module 6 — Saving without discounting inside 28 days, and when a discount is the right tool.
  • Module 7 — Live call teardowns: two saved, two lost.
  • Pass mark 85%, with a practical element: one role-played refusal call assessed against the scorecard.
  • Certification is recorded against the agent ID and gates campaign allocation, so it is visible in the dashboards from Pillar 3.
  • New starters certify before their first live retention call; existing agents recertify quarterly with an updated module reflecting that quarter's top refusal patterns.

Data, Dashboards & Performance Management

Owners: Data / Analytics, Retention Strategy, CS Leadership. Goal: replace lagging pause reports with leading indicators we can act on inside 72 hours.
Refined High impact Data

7. Multi-View Retention Dashboards

Two views, one data model. The refinement worth insisting on: pause data is a lagging indicator. By the time a pause is recorded we have already lost. Both views must carry leading indicators drawn from the transition tracker and the day-3 check-in.

Executive view

  • Box-2 retention versus the 47% target, and the gap expressed in boxes per week against 6.63.
  • Cohort survival curves by sign-up week, out to week 24.
  • Pause rate split pre-delivery, days 0–7, days 8–28, day 29+.
  • Reason mix over time, with food refusal and subscription tracked separately.
  • Week 6 to week 9 retention delta, to monitor the price cliff.
  • Retention by transition track, eating type and grams band.

Agent view

  • My cohorts: sales, 28-day and 84-day retention, versus team median.
  • My transition-compliance rate: baseline captured, grams confirmed, box-2 date stated.
  • My QA scorecard trend and certification status.
  • My save rate on early pauses, split by reason.
  • My at-risk list today, with the recommended next action on each.

Leading indicators (both views)

  • First-meal-logged rate within 48 hours of delivery.
  • Day-3 check-in response rate and answer mix.
  • Warm-serve adoption, self-reported.
  • Tracker completion through day 7 and day 14.
  • Recipe-swap requests as a share of active first-boxers.
Data prerequisites Previous-food field, eating-type field, transition track, paused-before-first-box flag, grams band collapsed from 42 segments to the agreed set, and a clean join between pause reason, recipe and texture. Without these the dashboards cannot answer the questions we actually have.
Refined Low cost CS Leadership

8. Internal Retention Newsletter

A short weekly internal note that turns the dashboards into behaviour. Keep it to one screen and a fixed format so it gets read.

  • Number of the week. How far we are from the 6.63 target, and the direction of travel.
  • Save of the week. A 60-second clip of a real food-refusal save, with the specific line that turned it.
  • Myth-buster of the week. One piece of transition science an agent can use verbatim today.
  • Pattern watch. The top refusal reason this week and the approved response.
  • Recognition. Top performers on retention-adjusted metrics, not raw sales volume.
Refined Handle with care CS Leadership + HR

9. Performance Accountability Standards

Clear thresholds, for example rotating an agent off the campaign for retraining if their retention rate sits below a benchmark for four consecutive weeks. The principle is right; three refinements stop it backfiring.

  • Use fair maths. Rolling four-week retention on a minimum cohort size (25 or more sales), normalised for lead source, trial length and plan mix. Judging an agent on 8 sales is noise, and rotating them off for noise destroys trust in the whole programme.
  • Anti-gaming clause. If retention alone drives consequences, agents will quietly avoid fussy cats and kibble-fed households — exactly the customers we most need to win. Track cohort mix per agent and adjust for difficulty, or pair the retention metric with a transition-compliance metric that rewards doing the right thing regardless of outcome.
  • Coach before you rotate. Week 2 triggers a QA deep-dive and a coaching plan, week 3 triggers side-by-side calls, week 4 triggers rotation. Nobody should be surprised by a rotation.
  • Make the route back explicit. Recertify plus five monitored calls at or above scorecard standard, and they are back on the campaign.

New Retention Ideas: Solving the First Bowl

Nine new bets, all aimed squarely at food acceptance in the first fourteen days. Each is framed so it can be tested against a control, because the whole point is to prove which of these moves the 6.63.

N1 High impact Product + Packaging

The Taste Flight: Box 1 as a Sampler, Box 2 Rebuilt Around the Winners

Ship Box 1 as a deliberate flavour-and-texture flight in smaller portions rather than a normal week's supply of one or two recipes, and put a scorecard on the fridge with it. The parent rates each recipe as loved, ate some or refused, and Box 2 is automatically rebuilt around whatever won.

  • Turns a pass/fail week into a game with a result. "Which one wins?" is a question a customer wants to answer; "did the cat eat it?" is a question they can fail.
  • Every rating is data. We finally learn which recipes and textures actually fail, per eating type and previous food.
  • It makes Box 2 feel earned and personalised, which directly attacks the day 7 and 14 "surprise box" pause spike.
  • Pair with the promise: if nothing won, we rebuild Box 2 free with different textures before anyone pays again.
Test
Taste-flight Box 1 versus standard Box 1, matched on plan size and previous food.
KPI
Day 0–14 pause rate, scorecard completion rate, box-2 retention.
N2 High impact Fast to ship Packaging + Ops

The Transition Starter Kit, Printed on the Box Itself

The best transition advice we have is currently sitting in an email nobody opens on day 4. Move it onto the physical product, where a worried pet parent standing at the fridge will actually see it.

  • Day-numbered sleeves or stickers on each tray: "Day 1 — a spoonful, warmed", "Day 3 — a quarter of the tray".
  • A bold "warm me first" cue on every pack, with the 10-second microwave or 10-minute warm-water instruction right there.
  • A portion-marked scoop or spoon, and the customer's own grams-per-day number printed on the box lid.
  • A shallow, clean tasting plate in Box 1, because plate hygiene and shape genuinely affect acceptance.
  • Three sachets of freeze-dried aroma topper to sprinkle on days 1–3, tapering to nothing by day 5 as the palate resets.
  • A single card with the 20-minute rule and one line: "refusing at first is habit, not preference."
Why it works
Warming is the cheapest, highest-leverage acceptance lever we have, and it is currently optional and easy to miss.
KPI
Warm-serve adoption, day-5 pause rate, refusal rate by day.
N3 High impact Commercial + CS

The Fussy Cat Promise: "Won't Eat It? You Won't Pay For It"

Right now, pausing is the only self-service tool a frustrated customer has, so they use it on day 5. Give them a better one and make it a headline promise rather than a buried policy.

  • Unlimited free recipe and texture swaps in the first 28 days.
  • If the cat rejects everything in Box 1, Box 2 is rebuilt free with different textures — one more genuine attempt at no cost.
  • If it still fails after a supported transition, refund the first box and part on good terms. We lose one box and keep the brand advocate.
  • Every touchpoint says it: "you will never pay for food your cat won't eat in the first month."
Model the cost before launch Cap swaps per household, require the day-3 check-in to be completed before a refund is granted, and monitor abuse. Judge the promise on contribution margin over 84 days, not on refund volume in isolation — it should also lift checkout conversion, which is where much of the payback sits.
N4 High impact Product + Data

The 14-Day Transition Tracker

A lightweight guided tool — in-app, or a no-login web page linked from SMS — that walks the household through their assigned track one day at a time and adapts to what they report.

  • One tap per meal: ate it all, ate some, sniffed it, refused. Tomorrow's plan adjusts automatically ("hold at a quarter today and warm it ten seconds longer").
  • A progress bar that treats partial acceptance as progress. This is the psychological core of the tool: it makes "ate some" feel like a win rather than a failure.
  • Day-appropriate reassurance, including the honest message that some kibble-fed cats take three to four weeks.
  • A live risk score pushed into the CRM and the agent dashboard, so two refusals in a row triggers help automatically. This is the leading indicator Pillar 3 is missing.
  • A shareable "we did it" moment at day 14 that doubles as social proof and a review prompt.
KPI
Tracker adoption, day-7 and day-14 completion, pause rate for trackers versus non-trackers.
N5 High impact Testable now CS + Ops

The Day-3 Rescue Drop

Surprise-and-delight aimed precisely at the day-5 cliff. Trigger: a negative day-3 check-in answer, two refusals logged in the tracker, or no meal logged at all within 48 hours of delivery.

  • Next-day dispatch of a small free rescue pack: a different texture, an aroma topper, a printed rescue plan, and something for the human (a decent cat treat, a fridge magnet with the plan on it).
  • A personal note referencing the specific problem they reported. Not a template.
  • An optional booked call slot rather than an unsolicited ring, which converts far better.
  • The point is timing above all. Arriving on day 4 with unexpected help, before the customer has decided to quit, is worth more than any save call on day 6.
Test
Rescue drop versus email-only support for matched at-risk customers.
KPI
Day 4–10 pause rate among flagged customers, cost per retained customer.
N6 Medium impact Low cost Data + Product

The One-Number Feeding Plan

We run 42 grams-per-day segments where Butternut runs 9, and 92.1% of our customers sit in just four bands. That complexity is invisible to us and confusing to them, and confusion about how much to serve looks exactly like a cat refusing food.

  • Collapse to a defensible set of bands anchored on 65g, 90g, 130g and 180g, with a clear rule for the tails.
  • Print one number and one sentence on the box: "Ziggy: 90g a day. That is one tray, split into two meals."
  • Agents confirm the number on the call and the customer repeats it back. It is a scorecard line for a reason.
  • Give fulfilment a single feeding-guidance standard so the pack, the email and the agent all say the same thing.
  • Revisit portion sizing at week 6 alongside the value bridge. Larger plans retain better, so right-size deliberately rather than by accident — while remembering the 180g sample is still small.
N7 High impact Fast to ship CRM + Product

The No-Surprises Box 2 Control Panel

Pause spikes land on days 7 and 14 — precisely when box 2 appears — and subscription concerns are the reason on around a quarter of 7-day-trial pauses. The gap between boxes is being experienced as an ambush.

  • Notify two days before dispatch, with the price and the contents stated plainly.
  • Offer one-tap "give us another week" date shifting, positioned as the normal thing to do while a cat is still settling.
  • Let them change recipes and textures in the same click, so the fix for "this one didn't work" is a swap, not a pause.
  • Make "skip one box" more prominent than "pause subscription" in the account area. A skip is recoverable; a pause usually is not.
  • Test whether the trial gap itself should flex with the transition track, giving kibble-fed cats a longer runway before box 2.
N8 Medium impact Brand + CS

The Transition Clinic and the Week Two Club

Authority and peer proof do work that a retention agent cannot do, because the customer does not believe the company has an impartial view.

  • Transition Clinic. A bookable 10-minute call or video slot with a qualified feline nutrition adviser or vet nurse, offered free to fussy-flagged and kibble-fed households in week one. "A nurse will help you" beats "an agent will call you".
  • Week Two Club. A short drip of real customer stories chosen for difficulty, not for triumph: cats who refused for nine days and now clear the bowl. Normalising a slow start is the cheapest intervention available to us.
  • Live group session. A weekly 20-minute open clinic for new customers. Hearing another owner describe the same problem does more than any email.
N9 Medium impact Research Product + R&D

The Texture Ladder and the Bridge Recipe

If food refusal is our biggest churn driver, some of the answer has to be the food itself rather than the messaging around it.

  • Map our recipes onto a texture ladder from most kibble-adjacent to most different (firmer and drier through to loose and gravy-rich), then assign a starting rung by previous food rather than letting the customer guess.
  • Investigate a deliberate bridge format for kibble converts: a firmer, drier, more aromatic chicken recipe designed as rung one, whose only job is to be accepted on day one.
  • Review whether an aroma topper should be a standing product line, given that fresh aroma is our stated advantage for fussy eaters.
  • Feed the refusal taxonomy from initiative 4 and the taste-flight scorecards from N1 into the recipe roadmap. If a specific recipe or texture dominates refusals, fix or retire it.

Transition Tracks: Tailored to the Previous Food

One question at sign-up drives everything downstream. These tracks come straight from the blog audit, and they are the operational backbone of initiative 1.

Previous food Track length What to tell the customer Day 1–3 mechanics Risk level
Dry / kibble 14 days, up to 3–4 weeks This is the longest switch there is, and that is normal. Kibble is coated in fat and flavour sprays after cooking, so your cat is unlearning a habit, not rejecting real food. Spoonful of warmed chicken on a clean plate. Remove kibble and treats between meals. 20-minute rule, then offer the usual food. Repeat day 1 if untouched. Highest
Wet (pouches / trays) 7–10 days Texture and moisture are already familiar; the palate needs to reset away from flavour digests. Unfamiliar is not the same as unwanted. Mix fresh into the old food and increase gradually. Warm to lift aroma. Go slowly to avoid digestive upset. Medium
Raw 3–7 days Same philosophy, gently cooked. Most cats adapt quickly because both are moist and meat-rich. Mix fresh into raw, increasing slowly. Serve from the fridge. Warm aroma helps. Lowest
Mixed feeding 10–14 days Replace the dry portion first, since that is the habit doing the damage, then move the wet meals across. Swap the wet meal to fresh first for an easy early win, then work on the dry meal. Medium to high
Any of the above, flagged fussy Add 7 days to the track We expect this to take longer and we have planned for it. Fresh aroma is on our side; patience is the strategy. Smallest possible portions, warmed every time, one recipe at a time, no pressure, offered at consistent times. Highest
Non-negotiable in every track Warm the food. Clean plate. Old food and treats removed between meals. Start with chicken because it is easiest on the stomach. Twenty minutes, then take it away. Introduce other recipes only once one is settled. Every cat moves at its own pace.

Key-Day Intervention Map

The CRM journey rebuilt around the pause data rather than a calendar. Risk levels reflect observed pause volume.

Day 0
Sale
Capture previous food, brand and eating type. Assign the transition track. State the grams per day and have it repeated back. State the box 2 date and price plainly. Set the expectation that week one is a tasting session, not a verdict. Never promise instant love.
Days 1–3
Pre-delivery — 23% of pauses
Fill the silence. Tracking, what is in the tasting box, how to prepare, and a plain restatement of how the subscription works. This window is buyer's remorse and subscription aversion, so reassurance about flexibility matters more than product education here.
Delivery day
Tasting session opens
SMS on delivery: warm it, clean plate, one spoonful, remove the old food, twenty minutes. Prompt the first meal log. If nothing is logged within 48 hours, flag it.
Day 3
Pre-empt the cliff
One-tap check-in: ate it all / ate some / sniffed / refused. "Sniffed" and "refused" trigger the rescue drop and an offer of a booked call or clinic slot. This is the single most important message in the entire journey, because it lands before the customer decides to quit.
Day 5
The cliff — peak pause day
Reassurance plus permission: partial eating on day 5 of a fourteen-day switch is on track. Show their own progress back to them. Offer a free recipe or texture swap explicitly, before they go looking for the pause button.
Days 6–7
Box 2 pre-notification
Two days before dispatch: price, contents, and one-tap options to shift the date, skip one box, or change recipes. Removes the ambush that drives the day-7 spike.
Days 8–13
Consolidation
Introduce recipe two now that one has settled. Confirm the portion is right. Early benefit spotting: litter tray, energy, coat. Keep the tracker moving.
Day 14
Second spike
Celebrate completion of the track and make it shareable. For cats still transitioning, extend the plan honestly — three to four weeks is within normal for a kibble convert — and offer the clinic.
Days 21–28
Habit formed
Lock in the routine, confirm the long-term recipe mix, right-size the plan, and ask for a review while sentiment is high. Last window where support beats pricing.
Weeks 6–7
Price cliff
Proof moment before the invoice changes: visible benefits, before-and-after, their own transition story. Then a tapered price step with right-sizing options. This is where a discount is the right tool, and the only place it is.

Guardrails: What We Will Not Do

No discounting inside 28 days Price is not the objection yet. A discount buys nothing, trains the customer to expect one, and makes the week-7 step worse.
No promising instant success "Cats love it" in the sales call is what manufactures the day-5 disappointment. We promise a supported switch, not an instant one.
No counting bought weeks as retention A commitment offer that guarantees six weeks proves nothing. Judge it at day 84.
No public shame lists Accountability runs through coaching, calibration and a clear route back, on cohorts large enough to be meaningful.
No metric that can be gamed by cherry-picking If retention alone drives consequences, agents avoid fussy cats and kibble households. Adjust for cohort difficulty.
No initiative without a measurement plan Every item on this page names a control group or a before-and-after window before it ships.

Open Decisions and Questions

  • Trial length. 14-day plans retain worse and 7-day plans generate more subscription-related pauses. Should the gap before box 2 flex with the transition track rather than being a single commercial choice?
  • Grams bands. What is the defensible replacement for 42 segments, and who owns the rule for customers outside the four dominant bands?
  • Plan size and retention. Larger plans retain better, but the 180g sample is small (70 box 2s, 157 sales). Is this a real effect or a household-size artefact? Needs a bigger sample before we bias acquisition toward larger plans.
  • Cost of the Fussy Cat Promise. Modelled refund and swap cost against the expected lift in checkout conversion and 84-day margin.
  • Week-7 taper shape. Two steps or three, and does the deeper upfront discount in the 6-week programme need a different curve entirely?
  • Pause reason taxonomy. Current options collapse distinct problems into "subscription" and "food". Can we split refusal into texture, aroma, recipe and portion confusion at source?
  • Tracker build versus buy. Is a no-login web tracker linked from SMS enough for a first version, avoiding an app dependency?
