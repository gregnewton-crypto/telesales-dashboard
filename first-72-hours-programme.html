The Goal

Eliminate early-stage customer pauses in the first three days by reframing how customers experience their first box, and by guiding them through the transition instead of leaving them to judge it alone.

The problem is not that cats dislike the food. It is that we let a fourteen-day process be judged in seventy-two hours, against a finish line nobody agreed to. More than half of all pauses happen inside the first week, and roughly a quarter arrive before a box has even been delivered — those customers are not reacting to anything, they are pausing because they are not sure their cat will eat it and nothing has happened yet to tell them otherwise.

So the programme runs on one insight: it is the same anxiety throughout, first in its anticipatory form and then in its observed form. It does five things. It builds an upfront structure that removes the hesitation before the box hits the doorstep — a plan in their hands and a published schedule of the support that is coming. It teaches agents to sell Box 1 as an exploration rather than a delivery. It puts a physical scorecard on the fridge so partial progress becomes visible and recordable. It rewires the CRM to read that scorecard and offer a recipe swap at the exact moment a pause is being considered. And it manages the 72 hours around first delivery deliberately, rather than leaving customers to survive them.

## The First 72 Hours - Early-Pause Prevention Programme

**Goal:** eliminate early-stage customer pauses (days 1-3) by reframing how customers experience their first box and guiding them through the transition process.

The problem is not that cats dislike the food. It is that we let a fourteen-day process be judged in seventy-two hours, against a finish line nobody agreed to. More than half of all pauses happen inside the first week, and roughly a quarter arrive before a box has even been delivered - those customers are not reacting to anything, they are pausing because they are not sure their cat will eat it and nothing has happened yet to tell them otherwise.

**One anxiety, two phases.** It is the same question throughout - "is my cat actually going to eat this?" - asked first as a prediction and later as a verdict. Read as subscription aversion, the pre-delivery pause is close to unfixable. Read as uncertainty about acceptance, it is one of the cheapest problems we have, because certainty is content and content ships in days.

**Core components:**

1. **Transition & Texture Training Deck** - internal enablement that shifts how agents frame Box 1, positioning it as an exploratory tasting and texture trial rather than a standard order, and gives them the texture vocabulary to coach with.
2. **In-Box Recipe Scorecard** - a physical card in Box 1 with a daily tracker: which recipe was served, chilled or warmed, how the cat reacted on a four-point scale, and any texture preference. Partial acceptance is printed as progress, not failure. The card is the customer's diary; the data reaches us through a one-tap reply in the daily message, because we ask and they tap rather than expecting them to scan something unprompted.
3. **Scorecard-Driven CRM Messaging** - email and SMS flows triggered by early usage signals rather than calendar days, designed to intercept a pause and offer a recipe or texture swap instead, personalised with what the scorecard already told us.
4. **Early-Pause Prevention (Days 1-3)** - the 72 hours around first delivery managed deliberately rather than survived, so knee-jerk pauses are prevented before the transition has had a fair chance.
4a. **The Pre-Arrival Readiness Pack** - the upfront structure that removes hesitation before the box hits the doorstep. A personalised switch plan in their hands within the hour, a published itinerary of every message we will send and when, a two-minute readiness checklist, and the safety net stated before they need it: swaps are free, and you never need to pause in order to change something.

**Signature metric:** the swap-to-pause ratio. Every recipe or texture swap we trigger instead of a pause is a retained customer. **First read:** pre-delivery pause rate for customers who opened their switch plan versus those who did not.

Defining the Window: Two Clocks, Not One

"Days 1–3" means two different things in our data, and conflating them is the fastest way to build a journey that fires at the wrong moment. Both clocks need covering, and the CRM has to know which one it is reading.

Clock A — the order clock

Day 0 is checkout. Days 1–3 here are the pre-delivery window: the customer has paid, nothing has arrived, and they have time to talk themselves out of it.

This is where roughly 23% of pauses come from, and they are pausing on a prediction rather than an experience. They are not sure their cat will eat it, they have just committed to repeat deliveries of it, and nothing has happened yet to reassure them. What helps is a plan in their hands and visible proof that support is already scheduled.

Clock B — the bowl clock

Day 1 is the first meal served. Days 1–3 here are the tasting window, and they end just before the day-5 pause spike on the order clock.

This is where the scorecard lives and where the food itself is being judged. Every message must be keyed off delivery confirmation, not the order date, or a "how did meal one go?" prompt will land while the box is still on a van.

Implementation warning Delivery dates vary, and trial length varies. If we trigger on order date plus N days, the journey will drift out of sync with reality for a large share of customers and the day-3 checkpoint — the most important message in the programme — will miss. Delivery confirmation must be a reliable event in the CRM before the flows in component 3 go live. If it is not, that is the first thing to fix.

One Anxiety, Two Phases

The two clocks look like two different problems. They are not. They are the same anxiety — "is my cat actually going to eat this?" — asked at two different moments, and that makes this whole programme more coherent than it first appears.

  Anticipatory — before the box lands Observed — after the first meal
The thought "I've committed to repeat deliveries of food she might refuse." "She refused it. I was right to worry."
What they know Nothing yet. They are pausing on a prediction. One or two data points, read as a verdict.
Reason we record Subscription, or buyer's remorse. Pet did not eat the food.
What actually removes it A plan they can hold, and proof that help is already scheduled. A next step that changes one variable, and a free swap.
Owning component Component 4a, the pre-arrival readiness pack. Components 2 and 3, the scorecard and the flows it drives.
Why this reading matters commercially Read as subscription aversion, the pre-delivery pause is close to unfixable: the only levers are commercial terms, and softening those costs margin on every customer to save a few. Read as uncertainty about acceptance, it is one of the cheapest problems we have — because certainty is content, and content ships in days. Same 23% of pauses, two completely different price tags on the fix. That is the argument for treating the pre-arrival window as the first thing we build, not the last.
Status of this claim The retention review records these pauses as remorse and subscription aversion; the anticipatory-acceptance reading is our working hypothesis, and the two are compatible — a subscription is only frightening when the outcome is uncertain. It is also testable, and cheap to test: if the hypothesis is right, customers who open their switch plan before delivery will pause pre-delivery at a measurably lower rate than those who do not. That comparison is the first read we should take from this programme.

The Reframe: A Trial, Not a Test

Every component below is a different delivery mechanism for one idea. Box 1 is not the first instalment of a standing order; it is a tasting and texture trial, and the customer is running it with us. That changes what success looks like on day two, and it changes what a bad reaction means: not "this failed", but "we have a result, let's use it".

  Box 1 as a standard order Box 1 as a tasting and texture trial
Success on day 2 The bowl is empty. We know something new about what this cat likes.
A refused meal A failure, and evidence the product is wrong. A data point that narrows down the recipe and texture.
Customer's job Passive. Wait and see whether it works. Active. Run the trial, log the reactions, tell us the winner.
Natural next step when it goes badly Pause the subscription. Swap the recipe or change the texture.
What box 2 is More of the same, arriving unbidden. The result of the trial, built around what won.
Emotional state on day 3 Anxious and out of pocket. Curious and mid-experiment.
The texture lever we already own, for free Our own product page says it plainly: serve straight from the fridge for a jelly-like texture, or warm it up to release a silky gravy. That means every tray we ship is already two textures, and a genuine texture trial costs us nothing — no new SKU, no extra shipment. A cat that refuses chilled jelly has not refused the recipe; it has refused one of the two ways we can serve it. Right now almost nobody in the first 72 hours knows that, which makes this the cheapest and fastest acceptance lever in the entire programme.

The framing script, and its failure modes

The reframe has to survive contact with a real call, so it is taught as specific language rather than a principle. The left column is what we hear today.

Don't say Say
"Cats love it, she'll be fine." "Most cats take a few days to switch, especially off dry food. Week one is about finding which recipe and texture she goes for."
"Your first box will arrive on Tuesday." "Tuesday is her tasting box — seven recipes to try, and two ways to serve each one. There's a card inside to keep score."
"Did she eat it?" "How did she approach it — did she sniff it, lick it, or take a few bites?"
"Just give it time." "Let's change one thing tonight: warm the next tray for ten seconds so the gravy comes through, and serve it on a clean plate."
"It's normal, cats are fussy." "Fussiness here is a habit from the old food, not a verdict on this one. It's the bit we're best at helping with."
"I can pause that for you now." "Before we pause anything — which ones has she tried? Let me swap the rest for the proteins that got the best reaction. That's free and it usually does it."
"I can give you 20% off the next box." "A discount won't make her eat it. Let's fix the food: different texture, different protein, and I'll send a topper to bridge the gap."

Assessment and rollout

  • Written quiz, 85% pass mark, weighted towards the transition tracks and the texture mechanics.
  • One assessed role-play: a bowl-day-2 refusal call, scored against the retention QA scorecard, with the reframe and the swap offer both required to pass.
  • Certification recorded against the agent ID and required before working live early-life contacts.
  • Delivered as two 45-minute sessions rather than one long one, with the practice session at least a day later so the language has time to settle.
  • Quarterly refresh slide built from the previous quarter's top three refusal patterns, so the deck stays current instead of ageing into a compliance exercise.

Design decisions worth defending

  • The four-point scale is the whole card. A binary "did they eat it?" manufactures failure on day one, which is precisely the behaviour we are trying to prevent. Four points make "sniffed it" a legitimate recordable outcome, and the card prints the interpretation directly on it so nobody has to guess whether a 2 is bad news.
  • Chilled and warmed are separate columns. This is what turns a recipe tracker into a texture tracker, and it costs a column. It also teaches the warming trick by implication: a parent looking at two boxes will try both.
  • The cat's name is handwritten, not printed. Variable-data printing adds cost and lead time, and a name the owner writes themselves creates more ownership than one we print. "Coming from" also captures the previous food at the moment they are most willing to tell us.
  • It lives on the fridge, not in the recycling. Sat on top of the trays under the lid so it is the first thing seen, with a magnet or magnetic strip so it ends up next to where the food is served. A card in a leaflet stack is a card nobody reads.
  • The card is the customer's diary, not our sensor. The card exists to make the trial real, to teach the two textures and to make partial acceptance visible to the person standing at the fridge. It is not how the data reaches us — see how the data reaches us below, because this is the dependency the whole programme rests on.

Reverse of the card

  • Their transition track and its honest length, selected from the previous food they were asked about at sign-up.
  • The first-meal method: a spoonful, warmed ten seconds, on a clean plate, old food and treats out of the way.
  • The 20-minute rule: if it is refused, take it away, offer the usual food, try again tomorrow. Framed as normal procedure, not as a contingency.
  • The two-texture trick, spelled out: chilled for jelly, warmed for gravy, and try both before writing off a recipe.
  • "Swap it, don't stop it" with the short link and QR, plus what a swap costs, which is nothing.
  • A finish line: complete seven days and we will build box 2 around the winners.
Operational questions to close before print Unit cost at volume and where it sits in the pack sequence. Whether the magnet is worth its cost or a fridge-safe adhesive strip will do. Print lead time against the pilot date. Whether Fulfilment can insert a track-specific reverse (four variants) or whether version one carries all four tracks on the same card. Only the last one changes the design meaningfully.

How the Data Reaches Us

If the card is filled in at home and stays there, we learn nothing, and component 3 has nothing to trigger on. This is the single biggest delivery risk in the programme, so it needs an answer before anything goes to print.

The card is the diary. The message is the sensor. A QR code requires the customer to start something: find their phone, open the camera, scan, land on a page, choose an answer. Three times a day, for a week, in a busy kitchen. That will not happen at the rate component 3 needs. So we invert it. We ask, they tap. The daily message arrives at the moment the meal happens and carries the four answers as four tappable options, so logging takes one thumb and no navigation. The card gives the customer their record and the framing; the reply gives us the data.

The capture routes, in order of how much we should rely on them

Route What the customer does What we get Rely on it for
1. One-tap reply in the daily SMS Taps one of four options in a message that just arrived. No app, no login, no typing. Recipe, reaction, timestamp. Enough to branch the journey. The primary sensor. Everything in component 3 should be designed to run on this alone.
2. Four buttons in the email Clicks one button. Same four labels as the card and the SMS. Same as above, for customers who live in email rather than messages. Coverage of the non-SMS cohort.
3. Printed short link and QR on the card Starts it themselves, at the fridge, when they want to log a full day. Richer entries: texture, notes, multiple meals. The engaged minority. Useful, never assumed.
4. Card photo at day 7 Sends one photo in reply to a message. Zero data entry. The whole week at once, including from customers who never logged digitally. Retrospective recipe and texture preference, and box-2 build. Too late to intervene.
5. Agent capture on any contact Reads their card out to whoever they are speaking to. A full history, plus the reasons behind it, which no form captures. The highest-value data we get, from the smallest number of customers.
6. Implicit signals Nothing. They just behave. Swap requests, portion changes, delivery shifts, pause-page visits — and silence. Risk scoring when no explicit log exists. Always available.
Do not ask people to log the same meal twice If the card says "write it here" and the SMS says "and tell us there", we have created duplicate work and most people will do neither. The way out is to give the two artifacts different jobs, in the customer's own terms: the card is for them, the tap is for us. Their record on the fridge is what makes progress visible; ours is what makes the next message useful. The four labels must be word-for-word identical across the card, the SMS and the email, or the two records will not reconcile and the customer will notice the seam.

What the one-tap reply actually looks like

SMS Trigger: delivery confirmed, evening of bowl-day 1 · the primary capture event

Marro: how did cat_name get on with the recipe_name? Tap the closest:

Cleared the plate Ate some Sniffed or licked Walked away

Each option is its own pre-filled link, so the tap is the log — it lands on a confirmation page with the next step already personalised to the answer, and never asks them to select anything or sign in.

Design the journey to work when the data does not arrive

Assume a meaningful share of customers will log nothing. The flows have to degrade gracefully rather than stall, so every stage runs one of two branches:

  • If we know — personalise hard. Name the recipe, name the reaction, give the specific next move. This is where the programme earns its keep.
  • If we don't know — do not guess and do not go quiet. Send the generic-but-useful version of the same message and make it a question, so the message doubles as another chance to capture a reaction.
  • Treat silence as a signal in its own right. No log by 48 hours after delivery is one of the strongest early risk flags available to us, because the engaged customers are the ones who answer. Non-loggers should be routed to the day-3 checkpoint and the rescue path regardless, not dropped because we lack data on them.
  • Never make an intervention conditional on complete data. One reaction on bowl-day 1 is enough to branch. Waiting for a full week of entries means intervening after the day-5 cliff, which is the failure we are trying to prevent.

What engineering needs to build

  • Tokenised one-tap links, one per option, scoped to a customer, a day and a recipe, and not guessable. No login, and safe to open on a shared device.
  • Idempotent writes. People tap twice, forward messages, and change their minds. Last answer for a given day and recipe wins; duplicates must not create two events.
  • Late and out-of-order entries. Someone logging Monday's meal on Wednesday should still land on Monday. Store the meal date, not just the submission time.
  • A single scorecard event schema shared by all routes, so an agent-entered reaction, a tap and a QR entry are indistinguishable downstream.
  • Agent-side entry in the CS tool, because route 5 is worthless if the agent has nowhere to put what they just heard.
  • Confirmation pages that do the next job, rather than saying "thanks". Each answer lands on the relevant next step, and for the two low answers, the free swap.
The number that tells us whether any of this worked Signal coverage: the share of first-box customers we get at least one reaction from, by any route, within 48 hours of delivery. It belongs on the dashboard from day one of the pilot, because it caps the effectiveness of every personalised branch in component 3. If coverage comes in low, the fix is the capture mechanism, not the copy — and we should find that out in the pilot rather than after a print run. We have no basis for predicting the rate yet, so the pilot's job is to measure it, not to hit a number someone guessed.

The design principle for every branch A pause is the only self-service tool most customers currently have, so of course they reach for it. Every message in this component exists to put a better tool within easier reach: a swap, a texture change, or a week's delay. If the swap is harder to find than the pause button, the flows will not work no matter how good the copy is.

Data the flows depend on

  • Delivery confirmation as a reliable, timely CRM event. Everything on clock B hangs off this.
  • Previous food and eating type, captured at sign-up, to select the track and to write honest timelines into the copy.
  • Scorecard events: recipe, serve temperature, reaction score, meal date. See how the data reaches us — the flows are only as good as this coverage.
  • A swap endpoint that can rebuild the remainder of box 1 and the contents of box 2 without an agent.
  • Pause-intent as an event, not just pause-completed, so the interception in the last row is possible at all.
4a

The Pre-Arrival Readiness Pack

Owners: Retention Strategy and CRM, with Brand for the plan copy. Format: a personalised switch plan, a published support itinerary, a readiness checklist and a stated safety net. No engineering beyond a templated page.
Core component High impact Ships fast Retention + CRM

If the pre-delivery pause is a pause on a prediction, the counter is to make the prediction unnecessary. Build the structure before the box hits the doorstep, so that by the time it arrives the customer already knows exactly what they are doing, already knows help is coming and when, and already knows what happens if it goes badly. Hesitation needs uncertainty to survive.

The pack makes three promises, in this order:

You have a plan Not general advice. A named, day-by-day plan for this cat, built from what they were eating last week, in the customer's hands within an hour of checkout.
Help is already scheduled Every message we will send, when it arrives and what it is for — published upfront, so support is a visible commitment rather than a hope.
Nothing is stuck The safety net stated before they need it: swaps are free, box 2 gets rebuilt around the winners, and you never have to pause in order to change something.

1. The Switch Plan

Sent within an hour of checkout and referenced by name on the sales call. A web page rather than an attachment, so we can update it, personalise it and — critically — measure whether it was opened.

  • Headed with the cat's name and what they are switching from, because that is what makes it read as a plan rather than a brochure.
  • The honest length for that baseline: 14 days off dry food, 7–10 off wet, 3–7 off raw, plus a week if they told us the cat is fussy.
  • Day 1, day 2 and day 3 spelled out as instructions, with a stated "what good looks like" for each so success is defined before it can be misjudged.
  • The two mechanics that do the most work: warm it for ten seconds to bring the gravy through, and the 20-minute rule.
  • One line of reassurance with a reason attached — early hesitation is a habit from food engineered to be craved, not a verdict on this food.

2. The Support Itinerary

The part that does the anxiety work. A customer who knows a checkpoint is coming on day 3 has no reason to make a decision on day 2. Publishing the schedule converts an unknown into an appointment.

3. The Two-Minute Readiness Checklist

Sent the day before delivery. Small, concrete asks that recruit the customer into the trial before the box arrives — a customer who has already done something for the switch is materially less likely to abandon it.

  • Move the kibble, treats and old food somewhere out of reach for the first few days.
  • Pick a clean, shallow plate and keep it for this. Shape and hygiene genuinely affect acceptance.
  • Decide which meal will be the first one, so it happens on purpose rather than in a rush.
  • Clear a shelf: it arrives chilled and needs the fridge.
  • When the box lands, the Tasting Card goes on the fridge door before anything else.

4. The Safety Net, Stated Upfront

Currently the customer discovers their options at the moment of frustration, by hunting through the account area — where the most visible option is the pause button. Saying it upfront costs nothing and changes what the pause button is for.

  • Recipe and texture swaps are free in the first 28 days, as many as it takes.
  • If nothing in box 1 lands, box 2 gets rebuilt with different textures before they pay for it again.
  • The next delivery can be pushed back a week with one tap, which is what most people actually want.
  • Stated plainly once, in the customer's words: you never need to pause in order to change something.
The design rule: confidence, not caveats There is a real way to get this wrong. Content that leads with "your cat may refuse this at first" transfers our risk onto an already-nervous customer and could increase pre-delivery pausing rather than reduce it — and if it appears pre-purchase, dent conversion too. Every line has to be about what we will do, not what might go wrong. "Here is how we get her onto it, step by step" reads as expertise. "She might not eat it" reads as a warning. Same information, opposite effect, and it is worth briefing Brand explicitly on the difference.

How we will know it worked

  • Plan open rate before delivery. If they never opened it, nothing else in this component happened.
  • Pre-delivery pause rate for plan-openers versus non-openers. The direct test of the hypothesis. Observational rather than causal, so treat a gap as encouraging rather than conclusive.
  • Randomised itinerary test. Same plan, with and without the published schedule of messages, to isolate whether the appointment effect is real or whether the plan alone does the work.
  • Checklist completion, self-reported. A proxy for commitment before the box arrives.
  • Contact rate before delivery. Should fall if the pack is answering the questions people were phoning in with. If it rises, the plan is raising questions it does not answer.

Early-Pause Prevention: The Day 1–3 Interventions

Owners: Retention Strategy with CRM and Customer Service. Format: an intervention map from checkout to bowl-day 3, plus the rescue path.
Core component High impact Retention + CS

Educate before and during the first delivery so expectations are managed rather than discovered, and so a knee-jerk pause never becomes the obvious move. The principle throughout: arrive before the doubt. Every intervention below is scheduled earlier than the moment it is designed to prevent.

Checkout
The expectation contract
Capture previous food and eating type — this is what makes the switch plan personal, so it is worth protecting on the call. State the transition length for that baseline out loud. Name the plan and say it is arriving today. Confirm the box 2 date and price plainly, and how to change either. Never promise instant love — the overpromise made here is what detonates on bowl-day 2.
Order days 1–3
Pre-delivery — 23% of pauses
The highest-risk window nobody currently owns, and the one where customers pause on a prediction rather than an experience. Do not fill it with tracking updates. Fill it with the readiness pack from component 4a: the switch plan within the hour, the published itinerary so they can see support is already scheduled, and the safety net stated before they need it. A customer who knows a checkpoint is coming on day 3 has no reason to decide anything on day 2.
Delivery minus 1
Prepare the house
The two-minute readiness checklist: move the old food and treats out of reach, choose a clean shallow plate, decide which meal will be the first. Small, concrete asks that recruit the customer into the trial before the box arrives, which is the point as much as the preparation itself.
Bowl-day 1
The first meal, done properly
A spoonful, warmed ten seconds, clean plate, old food away, 20 minutes then withdraw and offer the usual dinner. Log meal one. The most common day-one failure is not the cat, it is a full portion served cold next to a bowl of kibble.
Bowl-day 2
Change one variable
If day one went well, increase slightly and stay on the same recipe. If it did not, change exactly one thing — temperature first, because chilled to warmed is free and turns jelly into gravy. Changing three things at once teaches us nothing and overwhelms the customer.
Bowl-day 3
The checkpoint that prevents day 5
The single most important contact in the programme. Reflect their own logged progress back to them, restate the real finish line, and put the swap in front of them explicitly before they go looking for the pause button. Two low scores here triggers the rescue path: a free recipe swap dispatched next day, and the offer of a booked call rather than an unsolicited one.
Bowl-days 4–7
Hold the line to the cliff
Order-clock day 5 sits in here for most customers, so the tone stays "on track" rather than "how's it going?". Introduce recipe two once one has settled. Confirm the portion. Start naming early benefits the customer can verify themselves.
Box 2 minus 2 days
No surprises
Price, contents and date stated plainly, with one-tap options to swap recipes, shift the date or skip. The day-7 and day-14 pause spikes are an ambush problem, and this is the cheapest fix for them.
What we are not doing in this window No discounts. Price is not the objection inside 28 days, and a discount offered on bowl-day 2 both wastes margin and signals that we think the food is the problem. No unsolicited outbound calls before day 3 either — a booked slot converts far better than an interruption, and an unexpected call about whether the cat is eating can create the doubt it was meant to resolve.

Swap, Don't Stop: The Offer Ladder

One ladder, used by agents, by the CRM flows and by the self-service surfaces, so a customer gets the same answer wherever they ask. Start at the top and only move down. Nothing on this ladder costs margin until the very last rung.

Rung 1
Change the texture, not the food. Chilled for jelly, warmed for gravy. Free, instant, needs nothing from us, and resolves a meaningful share of "she won't touch it" without changing a single recipe.
Rung 2
Swap the recipes. Rebuild the rest of box 1 and the contents of box 2 around the proteins with the best reactions on the card. Free, self-serve, and it uses data the customer generated themselves, which makes it persuasive.
Rung 3
Send a rescue pack. Next-day dispatch of a different texture, an aroma topper and a printed rescue plan, triggered by two low scores. Arriving unasked on bowl-day 4 is worth more than any save call on day 6.
Rung 4
Push the next box back a week. Positioned as the normal thing to do while a cat is still settling, not as a concession. This is what most people actually want when they click pause.
Rung 5
Skip one box. Recoverable, visible, and far better for us than a pause. Should sit higher in the account area than the pause option does.
Rung 6
Book the transition clinic. Ten minutes with someone qualified for fussy-flagged and kibble-fed cats. Authority does work an agent cannot, because the customer assumes we are biased.
Rung 7
Pause — deliberately, with a date. If we get here, pause cleanly, capture the structured reason, and schedule a check-back. A pause with a plan is recoverable; a pause after a fight is not.
The one rule No discount at any rung inside the first 28 days. If the cat is not eating, a cheaper box of food the cat is not eating solves nothing, and it trains the customer to expect a discount at every future wobble. Discount authority belongs to the post-28-day price conversation, which is a different problem with a different owner.

Measurement

The signature metric for this programme is the swap-to-pause ratio: for every pause we take in the first 72 hours, how many recipe or texture swaps did we trigger instead? It goes up when the programme is working, and unlike a pause rate it tells us whether the mechanism — not just the mood — is doing its job.

Test design

  • Pull the baselines first. We do not currently have clean 72-hour pause rates on either clock, and setting targets before we have them would be guessing. First deliverable of the data workstream, before anything ships.
  • Hold back 20% on the existing journey for at least four full cohorts. Early-life retention is seasonal and cohort-sensitive; one good week proves nothing.
  • Split the card by fulfilment batch rather than by customer, since inserting a card for some customers and not others within the same batch is an operational headache and a source of error.
  • Isolate the components where you can. Training and CRM can be measured separately from the card. If they all launch together and the number moves, we will not know which of the four earned it, and we will not know what to double down on.
  • Read the leading indicators weekly, the pause rates monthly. Card activation and reaction trajectories move within days; retention curves need cohorts to mature.

Guardrails and Risks

No discounting inside 28 days Price is not the objection in this window. Fix the food, the texture or the timing instead.
No promising instant success Every overpromise at checkout is a pause on bowl-day 2. The deck treats this as a zero-score behaviour for a reason.
Confidence, not caveats Pre-arrival content says what we will do, never what might go wrong. Warning a nervous customer that their cat may refuse it could cause the very pause we are trying to prevent.
The card must not become a leaflet If it ships in a stack of inserts it will not be read, and components 3 and 4 lose the framing they depend on. Placement is a requirement, not a detail.
Never depend on the customer starting the log We ask, they tap. Any design that requires them to find their phone and scan something unprompted will not produce the data the flows need.
Swap cost needs a cap Free swaps are the core mechanic and also the main cost risk. Cap per household, model it before the pilot, and watch it weekly.
Don't trigger on the wrong clock Messages keyed to order date instead of delivery will miss the moments that matter and erode trust in the journey.
Don't measure with everything at once Four components launching together will move a number nobody can attribute. Sequence so each earns its own read.

Open Decisions

  • How personalised does the switch plan need to be at version one? Four static pages, one per transition track, with the cat's name merged in, would ship in days. A genuinely dynamic plan is better and slower. Recommendation is to ship the static four and measure the open rate before investing further.
  • Should the plan be visible before purchase? If pre-delivery hesitation is really about whether the cat will eat it, that doubt exists at checkout too, and showing the plan on the product page could lift conversion as well as retention. It also risks foregrounding difficulty at the worst possible moment, so it needs its own test rather than an assumption either way.
  • Card variants. One card carrying all four transition tracks, or four track-specific versions inserted by Fulfilment? Four is better for the customer and harder for operations.
  • Reaction scale wording. "Walked away / sniffed or licked / ate some / cleared the plate" is proposed. Worth a quick test with real customers, because these four labels carry most of the psychological weight of the programme.
  • Swap scope. Can a swap change the remainder of box 1, or only box 2? The first is far more persuasive on bowl-day 2 and materially harder operationally.
  • Pause-page interception. Needs engineering on the account surfaces. If that is slow, is an email-and-SMS-only version worth launching first, accepting that it will miss the customers who pause silently?
  • Capture channel. Do we have reliable mobile numbers and SMS consent for enough of the base to make the one-tap reply the primary sensor? If not, the email variant has to carry it, which is slower and lands less often at mealtime. This is worth checking before the card goes to print, because it changes what the card asks people to do.
  • Day-7 card photos. Read manually at pilot volume, which is fine and also genuinely informative. Worth deciding now whether we ever intend to automate it, because if not, the card layout does not need to be machine-readable and the design gets freer.
  • Logging incentive. Is "box 2 built around the winners" enough of a reason to log, or does completion need to unlock something? Careful: paying for data tends to produce data rather than truth.
  • Completion incentive. Does finishing seven days on the card earn something, and if so, does it need to be a physical item or is "box 2 built around the winners" reward enough on its own?
  • Trial length interaction. Should the gap before box 2 flex with the transition track, giving kibble-fed cats a longer runway? Currently a commercial decision made independently of the transition, which is why the day-7 spike exists.
