The Goal

Eliminate early-stage customer pauses in the first three days by reframing how customers experience their first box, and by guiding them through the transition instead of leaving them to judge it alone.

The problem is not that cats dislike the food. It is that we let a fourteen-day process be judged in seventy-two hours, against a finish line nobody agreed to. ## The First 72 Hours - Early-Pause Prevention Programme

**Goal:** eliminate early-stage customer pauses (days 1-3) by reframing how customers experience their first box and guiding them through the transition process.

The problem is not that cats dislike the food. It is that we let a fourteen-day process be judged in seventy-two hours, against a finish line nobody agreed to. More than half of all pauses happen inside the first week, roughly a quarter arrive before a box has even been delivered, and the reason given clusters hard around the pet not eating.

**Four core components:**

1. **Transition & Texture Training Deck** - internal enablement that shifts how agents frame Box 1, positioning it as an exploratory tasting and texture trial rather than a standard order, and gives them the texture vocabulary to coach with.
2. **In-Box Recipe Scorecard** - a physical card in Box 1 with a daily tracker: which recipe was served, chilled or warmed, how the cat reacted on a four-point scale, and any texture preference. Partial acceptance is printed as progress, not failure.
3. **Scorecard-Driven CRM Messaging** - email and SMS flows triggered by early usage signals rather than calendar days, designed to intercept a pause and offer a recipe or texture swap instead, personalised with what the scorecard already told us.
4. **Early-Pause Prevention (Days 1-3)** - expectation setting before and during first delivery, so the critical 72-hour window is managed rather than survived, and knee-jerk pauses are prevented before the transition has had a fair chance.

**Signature metric:** the swap-to-pause ratio. Every recipe or texture swap we trigger instead of a pause is a retained customer.

Why the First 72 Hours

This is the evidence that makes a 72-hour programme the highest-value thing we can build. All of it comes from the D2MS retention review and the blog content audit.

The arithmetic worth remembering We are not trying to fix retention in the abstract. We need about seven households a week to make a different decision in their first few days. That is a small enough number to be winnable with better framing and a free recipe swap, and a specific enough number to tell us honestly whether this programme worked.

Defining the Window: Two Clocks, Not One

"Days 1–3" means two different things in our data, and conflating them is the fastest way to build a journey that fires at the wrong moment. Both clocks need covering, and the CRM has to know which one it is reading.

Clock A — the order clock

Day 0 is checkout. Days 1–3 here are the pre-delivery window: the customer has paid, nothing has arrived, and they have time to reconsider.

This is where roughly 23% of pauses come from, and the driver is remorse and subscription anxiety rather than food. Nothing about texture helps here. What helps is confirming what they bought, when it arrives, how flexible it is, and what week one will actually look like.

Clock B — the bowl clock

Day 1 is the first meal served. Days 1–3 here are the tasting window, and they end just before the day-5 pause spike on the order clock.

This is where the scorecard lives and where the food itself is being judged. Every message must be keyed off delivery confirmation, not the order date, or a "how did meal one go?" prompt will land while the box is still on a van.

Implementation warning Delivery dates vary, and trial length varies. If we trigger on order date plus N days, the journey will drift out of sync with reality for a large share of customers and the day-3 checkpoint — the most important message in the programme — will miss. Delivery confirmation must be a reliable event in the CRM before the flows in component 3 go live. If it is not, that is the first thing to fix.

The Reframe: A Trial, Not a Test

Every component below is a different delivery mechanism for one idea. Box 1 is not the first instalment of a standing order; it is a tasting and texture trial, and the customer is running it with us. That changes what success looks like on day two, and it changes what a bad reaction means: not "this failed", but "we have a result, let's use it".

  Box 1 as a standard order Box 1 as a tasting and texture trial
Success on day 2 The bowl is empty. We know something new about what this cat likes.
A refused meal A failure, and evidence the product is wrong. A data point that narrows down the recipe and texture.
Customer's job Passive. Wait and see whether it works. Active. Run the trial, log the reactions, tell us the winner.
Natural next step when it goes badly Pause the subscription. Swap the recipe or change the texture.
What box 2 is More of the same, arriving unbidden. The result of the trial, built around what won.
Emotional state on day 3 Anxious and out of pocket. Curious and mid-experiment.
The texture lever we already own, for free Our own product page says it plainly: serve straight from the fridge for a jelly-like texture, or warm it up to release a silky gravy. That means every tray we ship is already two textures, and a genuine texture trial costs us nothing — no new SKU, no extra shipment. A cat that refuses chilled jelly has not refused the recipe; it has refused one of the two ways we can serve it. Right now almost nobody in the first 72 hours knows that, which makes this the cheapest and fastest acceptance lever in the entire programme.

Transition & Texture Training Deck

Owners: Training and Retention Strategy, with Brand for the science content. Audience: sales and customer service agents. Format: internal deck plus assessment.
Core component High impact No engineering Training

An internal deck covering the transition phase and texture nuance, whose single behavioural objective is to change how agents frame Box 1 on the call. Everything else in the deck exists to make that reframe credible, because an agent who does not believe the science will not deliver the framing convincingly.

The framing script, and its failure modes

The reframe has to survive contact with a real call, so it is taught as specific language rather than a principle. The left column is what we hear today.

Don't say Say
"Cats love it, she'll be fine." "Most cats take a few days to switch, especially off dry food. Week one is about finding which recipe and texture she goes for."
"Your first box will arrive on Tuesday." "Tuesday is her tasting box — seven recipes to try, and two ways to serve each one. There's a card inside to keep score."
"Did she eat it?" "How did she approach it — did she sniff it, lick it, or take a few bites?"
"Just give it time." "Let's change one thing tonight: warm the next tray for ten seconds so the gravy comes through, and serve it on a clean plate."
"It's normal, cats are fussy." "Fussiness here is a habit from the old food, not a verdict on this one. It's the bit we're best at helping with."
"I can pause that for you now." "Before we pause anything — which ones has she tried? Let me swap the rest for the proteins that got the best reaction. That's free and it usually does it."
"I can give you 20% off the next box." "A discount won't make her eat it. Let's fix the food: different texture, different protein, and I'll send a topper to bridge the gap."

Assessment and rollout

  • Written quiz, 85% pass mark, weighted towards the transition tracks and the texture mechanics.
  • One assessed role-play: a bowl-day-2 refusal call, scored against the retention QA scorecard, with the reframe and the swap offer both required to pass.
  • Certification recorded against the agent ID and required before working live early-life contacts.
  • Delivered as two 45-minute sessions rather than one long one, with the practice session at least a day later so the language has time to settle.
  • Quarterly refresh slide built from the previous quarter's top three refusal patterns, so the deck stays current instead of ageing into a compliance exercise.

In-Box Recipe Scorecard

Owners: Product and Packaging with Brand design, Fulfilment for insertion. Audience: pet parents. Format: printed card in Box 1, with a digital twin.
Core component High impact Print lead time Product + Packaging

A physical card in the first box carrying a daily tracker: which recipe was served, how it was served, how the cat reacted, and what that says about texture preference. It does three jobs at once. It makes the trial framing tangible the moment the box is opened, it converts a vague worry into a recorded observation, and it produces the only early data we currently have no way of collecting.

Indicative design for the front of the card. Print artwork to be produced by Brand design.

Design decisions worth defending

  • The four-point scale is the whole card. A binary "did they eat it?" manufactures failure on day one, which is precisely the behaviour we are trying to prevent. Four points make "sniffed it" a legitimate recordable outcome, and the card prints the interpretation directly on it so nobody has to guess whether a 2 is bad news.
  • Chilled and warmed are separate columns. This is what turns a recipe tracker into a texture tracker, and it costs a column. It also teaches the warming trick by implication: a parent looking at two boxes will try both.
  • The cat's name is handwritten, not printed. Variable-data printing adds cost and lead time, and a name the owner writes themselves creates more ownership than one we print. "Coming from" also captures the previous food at the moment they are most willing to tell us.
  • It lives on the fridge, not in the recycling. Sat on top of the trays under the lid so it is the first thing seen, with a magnet or magnetic strip so it ends up next to where the food is served. A card in a leaflet stack is a card nobody reads.
  • The QR is the telemetry, the paper is the habit. Do not fight the paper. Let the card be the daily ritual and use the QR to pull the same data into the CRM, where component 3 needs it. Every scan should pre-fill from what we already know so logging a meal takes one tap.

Reverse of the card

  • Their transition track and its honest length, selected from the previous food they were asked about at sign-up.
  • The first-meal method: a spoonful, warmed ten seconds, on a clean plate, old food and treats out of the way.
  • The 20-minute rule: if it is refused, take it away, offer the usual food, try again tomorrow. Framed as normal procedure, not as a contingency.
  • The two-texture trick, spelled out: chilled for jelly, warmed for gravy, and try both before writing off a recipe.
  • "Swap it, don't stop it" with the short link and QR, plus what a swap costs, which is nothing.
  • A finish line: complete seven days and we will build box 2 around the winners.
Operational questions to close before print Unit cost at volume and where it sits in the pack sequence. Whether the magnet is worth its cost or a fridge-safe adhesive strip will do. Print lead time against the pilot date. Whether Fulfilment can insert a track-specific reverse (four variants) or whether version one carries all four tracks on the same card. Only the last one changes the design meaningfully.

The design principle for every branch A pause is the only self-service tool most customers currently have, so of course they reach for it. Every message in this component exists to put a better tool within easier reach: a swap, a texture change, or a week's delay. If the swap is harder to find than the pause button, the flows will not work no matter how good the copy is.

Data the flows depend on

  • Delivery confirmation as a reliable, timely CRM event. Everything on clock B hangs off this.
  • Previous food and eating type, captured at sign-up, to select the track and to write honest timelines into the copy.
  • Scorecard events: recipe, serve temperature, reaction score, timestamp.
  • A swap endpoint that can rebuild the remainder of box 1 and the contents of box 2 without an agent.
  • Pause-intent as an event, not just pause-completed, so the interception in the last row is possible at all.

Early-Pause Prevention: The Day 1–3 Interventions

Owners: Retention Strategy with CRM and Customer Service. Format: an intervention map from checkout to bowl-day 3, plus the rescue path.
Core component High impact Retention + CS

Educate before and during the first delivery so expectations are managed rather than discovered, and so a knee-jerk pause never becomes the obvious move. The principle throughout: arrive before the doubt. Every intervention below is scheduled earlier than the moment it is designed to prevent.

Checkout
The expectation contract
Capture previous food and eating type. State the transition length for that baseline out loud. Confirm the box 2 date and price plainly, and how to change either. Introduce the Tasting Card in one sentence so it is expected rather than discovered. Never promise instant love — the overpromise made here is what detonates on bowl-day 2.
Order days 1–3
Pre-delivery silence — 23% of pauses
The highest-risk window nobody currently owns. The customer has paid and has nothing to hold. Fill it with tracking, what is in the tasting box, how to prepare, and a plain restatement of how flexible the subscription is. This window is about remorse and commitment anxiety, so reassurance about control beats education about food.
Delivery minus 1
Prepare the house
One SMS with two asks: move the old food and treats out of the way, and choose a clean, shallow plate. Small, concrete, and it recruits the customer into the trial before the box arrives.
Bowl-day 1
The first meal, done properly
A spoonful, warmed ten seconds, clean plate, old food away, 20 minutes then withdraw and offer the usual dinner. Log meal one. The most common day-one failure is not the cat, it is a full portion served cold next to a bowl of kibble.
Bowl-day 2
Change one variable
If day one went well, increase slightly and stay on the same recipe. If it did not, change exactly one thing — temperature first, because chilled to warmed is free and turns jelly into gravy. Changing three things at once teaches us nothing and overwhelms the customer.
Bowl-day 3
The checkpoint that prevents day 5
The single most important contact in the programme. Reflect their own logged progress back to them, restate the real finish line, and put the swap in front of them explicitly before they go looking for the pause button. Two low scores here triggers the rescue path: a free recipe swap dispatched next day, and the offer of a booked call rather than an unsolicited one.
Bowl-days 4–7
Hold the line to the cliff
Order-clock day 5 sits in here for most customers, so the tone stays "on track" rather than "how's it going?". Introduce recipe two once one has settled. Confirm the portion. Start naming early benefits the customer can verify themselves.
Box 2 minus 2 days
No surprises
Price, contents and date stated plainly, with one-tap options to swap recipes, shift the date or skip. The day-7 and day-14 pause spikes are an ambush problem, and this is the cheapest fix for them.
What we are not doing in this window No discounts. Price is not the objection inside 28 days, and a discount offered on bowl-day 2 both wastes margin and signals that we think the food is the problem. No unsolicited outbound calls before day 3 either — a booked slot converts far better than an interruption, and an unexpected call about whether the cat is eating can create the doubt it was meant to resolve.

Swap, Don't Stop: The Offer Ladder

One ladder, used by agents, by the CRM flows and by the self-service surfaces, so a customer gets the same answer wherever they ask. Start at the top and only move down. Nothing on this ladder costs margin until the very last rung.

Rung 1
Change the texture, not the food. Chilled for jelly, warmed for gravy. Free, instant, needs nothing from us, and resolves a meaningful share of "she won't touch it" without changing a single recipe.
Rung 2
Swap the recipes. Rebuild the rest of box 1 and the contents of box 2 around the proteins with the best reactions on the card. Free, self-serve, and it uses data the customer generated themselves, which makes it persuasive.
Rung 3
Send a rescue pack. Next-day dispatch of a different texture, an aroma topper and a printed rescue plan, triggered by two low scores. Arriving unasked on bowl-day 4 is worth more than any save call on day 6.
Rung 4
Push the next box back a week. Positioned as the normal thing to do while a cat is still settling, not as a concession. This is what most people actually want when they click pause.
Rung 5
Skip one box. Recoverable, visible, and far better for us than a pause. Should sit higher in the account area than the pause option does.
Rung 6
Book the transition clinic. Ten minutes with someone qualified for fussy-flagged and kibble-fed cats. Authority does work an agent cannot, because the customer assumes we are biased.
Rung 7
Pause — deliberately, with a date. If we get here, pause cleanly, capture the structured reason, and schedule a check-back. A pause with a plan is recoverable; a pause after a fight is not.
The one rule No discount at any rung inside the first 28 days. If the cat is not eating, a cheaper box of food the cat is not eating solves nothing, and it trains the customer to expect a discount at every future wobble. Discount authority belongs to the post-28-day price conversation, which is a different problem with a different owner.

Measurement

The signature metric for this programme is the swap-to-pause ratio: for every pause we take in the first 72 hours, how many recipe or texture swaps did we trigger instead? It goes up when the programme is working, and unlike a pause rate it tells us whether the mechanism — not just the mood — is doing its job.

Test design

  • Pull the baselines first. We do not currently have clean 72-hour pause rates on either clock, and setting targets before we have them would be guessing. First deliverable of the data workstream, before anything ships.
  • Hold back 20% on the existing journey for at least four full cohorts. Early-life retention is seasonal and cohort-sensitive; one good week proves nothing.
  • Split the card by fulfilment batch rather than by customer, since inserting a card for some customers and not others within the same batch is an operational headache and a source of error.
  • Isolate the components where you can. Training and CRM can be measured separately from the card. If they all launch together and the number moves, we will not know which of the four earned it, and we will not know what to double down on.
  • Read the leading indicators weekly, the pause rates monthly. Card activation and reaction trajectories move within days; retention curves need cohorts to mature.

Guardrails and Risks

No discounting inside 28 days Price is not the objection in this window. Fix the food, the texture or the timing instead.
No promising instant success Every overpromise at checkout is a pause on bowl-day 2. The deck treats this as a zero-score behaviour for a reason.
The card must not become a leaflet If it ships in a stack of inserts it will not be read, and components 3 and 4 lose the data they run on. Placement is a requirement, not a detail.
Swap cost needs a cap Free swaps are the core mechanic and also the main cost risk. Cap per household, model it before the pilot, and watch it weekly.
Don't trigger on the wrong clock Messages keyed to order date instead of delivery will miss the moments that matter and erode trust in the journey.
Don't measure with everything at once Four components launching together will move a number nobody can attribute. Sequence so each earns its own read.

Open Decisions

  • Card variants. One card carrying all four transition tracks, or four track-specific versions inserted by Fulfilment? Four is better for the customer and harder for operations.
  • Reaction scale wording. "Walked away / sniffed or licked / ate some / cleared the plate" is proposed. Worth a quick test with real customers, because these four labels carry most of the psychological weight of the programme.
  • Swap scope. Can a swap change the remainder of box 1, or only box 2? The first is far more persuasive on bowl-day 2 and materially harder operationally.
  • Pause-page interception. Needs engineering on the account surfaces. If that is slow, is an email-and-SMS-only version worth launching first, accepting that it will miss the customers who pause silently?
  • Digital twin scope. Is a no-login QR page with one-tap logging enough for version one, avoiding an app dependency entirely?
  • Completion incentive. Does finishing seven days on the card earn something, and if so, does it need to be a physical item or is "box 2 built around the winners" reward enough on its own?
  • Trial length interaction. Should the gap before box 2 flex with the transition track, giving kibble-fed cats a longer runway? Currently a commercial decision made independently of the transition, which is why the day-7 spike exists.
