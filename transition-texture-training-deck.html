A. Why this matters · the point of the whole deck

You are not selling food.
You are setting a finish line.

Whatever you tell a customer to expect is what they will measure day five against. Set the wrong finish line and a perfectly normal transition looks like a failure.

Speaker notes

Pause here. This is the sentence you want people to leave with, and everything after this is just how to do it well.

Worth saying plainly: nobody is accusing anyone of doing a bad job. Most of us set the wrong finish line because it feels like good service to be reassuring. "She'll love it" is meant kindly. It just costs us the customer on day five.

B. The science, plainly

What fresh food actually is

  • Real, named meat and fish — you can see the protein and the percentage.
  • Gently cooked at around 90°C, then chilled or frozen rather than made shelf-stable.
  • High moisture, like wet food, so hydration comes with the meal.
  • Complete and balanced by design, vet approved, suitable for kittens through to seniors.
  • Prebiotics — MOS, FOS and psyllium seed — for gut health.

90°C is the number worth remembering: hot enough to kill bacteria, cool enough to leave taurine and amino acids intact.

Speaker notes

You do not need to be a nutritionist. You need four or five facts you can say with confidence, because confidence is what the customer is actually buying in week one.

If you only remember one: 90°C versus 150°C. That single comparison answers most of the questions you will get.

B. The science, plainly

What kibble actually is

  • Ingredients mixed into a dough and forced through a machine at over 150°C under pressure.
  • Around 10% moisture. Between 25% and 50% carbohydrate.
  • Nutrients like taurine are destroyed by that heat, then added back synthetically.
  • And then the part that matters most to us: it is sprayed with fat and flavour enhancers after cooking.

That spray is not an accident. It is there to make the food irresistible. Cats are conditioned to want it.

Speaker notes

Do not turn this into an attack on kibble or on the customer's past choices. They chose it in good faith and often for years. The line to hold is the one from our blog: kibble isn't poison, fresh isn't magic, but fresh is the more natural upgrade.

The flavour spray is the fact that unlocks the whole call, so make sure everyone can say it out loud before you move on.

B. The science, plainly · learn this one word for word

"Refusing it at first is habit, not preference."

This one line turns "my cat hates your food" into "my cat is still hooked on the old stuff". The first is a verdict you cannot argue with. The second is a problem you can help them solve.

Speaker notes

Get everyone to say it out loud. It feels awkward the first time and completely natural by the third.

The follow-up sentence matters as much as the line itself: "and that's the bit we're best at helping with." Never leave the customer holding the diagnosis on their own.

B. The science, plainly

Three myths you will hear this week

What they say What you say
"Kibble keeps her teeth clean" Crunching shifts a bit of surface plaque, but does nothing below the gumline where dental disease actually starts. The British Veterinary Association doesn't recommend dry food for dental care. Brushing and vet checks are the only proven things.
"She loves her kibble, so it must suit her" She loves the fat and flavour sprayed on after cooking. That is a conditioned response, not a nutritional choice — it's the feline version of ultra-processed food.
"Premium kibble is basically the same as fresh" Premium is genuinely better than budget: more meat, better sourcing. But it is still extruded above 150°C and still around 10% moisture, with nutrients added back afterwards.
Speaker notes

Tone is everything here. Never sneer at the old food, and never make the customer feel they've been getting it wrong for six years. You are adding information, not correcting them.

The dental one comes up most, usually from customers who were told it by someone they trust. Cite the BVA rather than us — it lands better coming from a third party.

B. The science, plainly

The other two conversations

Coming from wet food
  • Cooked at 121°C+ to make it shelf-stable.
  • The "4% meat rule": "with chicken" can legally mean 4% chicken.
  • Wet can be complete or complementary — a meal or a topper. Always check.
  • Many pouches use engineered flavour "digests" for the same reason kibble uses sprays.
Coming from raw
  • Same philosophy as us: real meat, high moisture, minimal processing.
  • The difference is cooking and handling, not intention.
  • The UK FSA found harmful bacteria in 35% of raw products tested.
  • Fresh is raw's philosophy made safer and easier — that is the whole pitch.
Speaker notes

Practical point: these two customers are much easier wins than kibble converts, because the texture and moisture are already familiar. A wet-food switch takes 7–10 days and a raw switch often 3–7.

With raw feeders, lead with respect. They have usually put real effort into their cat's diet. The argument is household safety and convenience, never nutrition.

C. The reframe

Box 1 is not a delivery

Stop treating it as

The first instalment of a standing order. Food arrives, cat eats it, subscription continues.

If the cat doesn't eat it, the order was wrong. The only lever is stop.

Start treating it as

A tasting and texture trial. Several recipes, two ways to serve each one, and one job: find out what this cat goes for.

If a recipe doesn't land, we have a result. The lever is swap.

Say "tasting box", not "first box". It costs you one word and it changes what the customer thinks they are being sent.

Speaker notes

The wording change is small and it does a lot of work. "Your first box arrives Tuesday" sets up an expectation of normal service. "Tuesday is her tasting box" sets up an experiment, and experiments cannot fail on day two — they only produce results.

Ask the room for the last time a customer said "she didn't eat it" and how they replied. Use a real answer to show how differently that call goes under the two framings.

C. The reframe

Two finish lines, same cat

If day 5 means "cleared the bowl"

Most customers fail. Failure feels like a wasted month coming. So they pause.

If day 5 means "tasted it and interested"

Most customers pass. Passing feels like progress. So they carry on.

Same cat, same bowl, same amount eaten. The only thing that changed is what we told them to expect — and we are the ones who told them.

Speaker notes

This is the slide to return to whenever someone asks whether the framing really matters. It costs nothing, changes no product, and flips the same set of facts from failure to progress.

Concretely, week one success is: sniffed it, licked it, ate some of the chicken. Say those words on the call so the customer has a target they can actually hit.

C. The reframe

What we promise, and what we never promise

Promise freely
  • A plan built around what her cat is eating right now.
  • Support at the moments that matter, including a day-3 check-in.
  • Free recipe and texture swaps for the first 28 days.
  • Box 2 built around whatever wins.
Never, under any circumstances
  • "She'll love it."
  • "She'll eat it straight away."
  • "Cats always take to this one."
  • "It'll be easy."
Speaker notes

Be direct about this: overpromising is scored as an automatic fail on the retention QA scorecard. Not because it is dishonest in spirit — people say it to be warm — but because it is the single most reliable way to create a day-5 pause.

Everything in the left column is a promise we can actually keep, and each one is more reassuring than "she'll love it" because it is specific.

D. Texture nuance · the cheapest thing we own

Every tray is two textures

Straight from the fridge, it is jelly-like. Warmed for ten seconds, it releases a silky gravy. Same tray, same recipe, completely different experience for the cat.

A cat that turns down chilled jelly has not refused the recipe. She has refused one of the two ways we can serve it — and almost nobody in their first week knows the other one exists.

Speaker notes

This is the highest-value slide in the deck for day-to-day calls. It costs us nothing: no new product, nothing to ship, no refund. Just a sentence.

Warming also lifts the aroma, and aroma is most of how a cat decides. Ten seconds in the microwave, or sit the tray in warm water for ten minutes. Never hot — warm.

D. Texture nuance

The texture ladder

1
Firmest and driest — closest to what a kibble-fed cat recognises. Serve chilled, small amount, on a flat plate.
2
Flaked and shredded, still fairly firm, lightly warmed so there is a little gravy but the texture holds.
3
Softer, more gravy — warmed properly. Familiar territory for a cat coming off wet pouches.
4
Loosest and most gravy-rich — fully warmed and stirred. Best for cats who already like moisture, and for seniors.

Start where the cat already is, then move one rung at a time.

Speaker notes

The rungs are about how you serve it as much as which recipe you pick. Temperature and stirring change the texture more than most people expect.

The discipline that matters: change one thing at a time. If a customer changes recipe, temperature and portion all at once and it works, we have learned nothing and neither have they.

D. Texture nuance

Give them the picture

Vague — leaves them guessing
  • "It's a bit different to what she's used to."
  • "It's got some jelly in it."
  • "It's quite meaty."
  • "Just warm it up a bit."
Specific — they can picture it
  • "Jelly-like straight from the fridge."
  • "Warm it ten seconds and it turns to a silky gravy."
  • "Shredded and flaked, rather than a smooth pâté."
  • "You'll see real pieces of chicken, not a paste."

Texture is the one thing a customer genuinely cannot imagine before the box arrives. Describe it and you remove a whole category of surprise.

Speaker notes

Fussy-eater households are usually fussy about texture rather than flavour, and they often already know it — "she won't eat anything with jelly", "she only likes pâté". If you use precise words, they will tell you exactly what to send.

Write down what they say. It is the most useful thing you will capture on the whole call.

D. Texture nuance

Match the first serve to the last meal

Coming from First serve Why
Dry / kibble A spoonful of chicken, warmed, on a clean plate. Old food and treats out of the way. Aroma has to do the persuading, because the texture is completely new.
Wet pouches Chilled or barely warmed, mixed into the old food and increased slowly. Texture is already familiar. The palate just needs to reset off flavour digests.
Raw Straight from the fridge, mixed into the raw, increasing gradually. Both are moist and meat-rich, so most cats barely notice.
Mixed feeding Replace the wet meal first, then work on the dry one. Take the easy win first. It builds the customer's confidence before the hard part.
Speaker notes

Note the mixed-feeding row, because it is the one people get wrong. Instinct says tackle the dry food first since it is the problem. In practice, swapping the wet meal gives you a win on day one, and a customer who has seen one win will persist through a harder second week.

D. Texture nuance

The three mechanics that do most of the work

1

Warm it

Ten seconds in the microwave, or the tray in warm water for ten minutes. Lifts the aroma and turns jelly to gravy.

2

Clean, shallow plate

Not the usual bowl, not next to the kibble. Old food and treats moved out of reach between meals.

3

Twenty minutes

Then take it away and offer her usual dinner. Try again tomorrow. This is normal procedure, not a fallback.

The most common day-one failure isn't the cat. It's a full portion, served cold, next to a bowl of kibble.

Speaker notes

Teach all three, every time, on every first-box call. They take fifteen seconds to say and they are the difference between a day-one refusal and a day-one nibble.

The 20-minute rule needs framing carefully. Presented as "if it fails, here's what to do" it plants doubt. Presented as "here's how the first few days work" it sounds like expertise. Same instruction, opposite effect.

E. Transition tracks · the one question

"What's she eating at the moment?"

The track, the timeline, the first texture, the first recipe, what goes in box 2, and every message she gets for the next fortnight — all of it comes out of the answer to that one question.

Speaker notes

It is a required field, and it is scored on the QA scorecard. Do not guess it, do not infer it from the brand they mentioned in passing, and do not skip it because the call is running long. Ask, and get the eating type too: easy, normal, or fussy.

Frame it as being for the cat's benefit, not as data collection: "so I can tell you exactly how to do the switch for her."

E. Transition tracks

The four tracks

Coming from How long, honestly What you tell them
Dry / kibble 14 days, sometimes 3–4 weeks "This is the longest switch there is, and that's normal. She's unlearning a habit."
Wet 7–10 days "Texture's already familiar, so this is usually a quick one."
Raw 3–7 days "Same sort of food, just gently cooked. Most cats barely blink."
Mixed 10–14 days "We'll do the wet meal first for a quick win, then the dry."
Flagged fussy Add a week to any of the above "We plan for longer with fussy ones. Patience is genuinely the strategy."
Speaker notes

Say the number out loud on the call. Every time. A customer who has heard "two weeks" is a customer who is still going on day five.

The fussy row is not a get-out. It is a promise that we have planned for the harder version of their situation, which is exactly what an anxious owner of a fussy cat wants to hear.

E. Transition tracks

The kibble track, days one to three

Day 1

Start small

A spoonful of warmed chicken on a clean plate. Old food and treats removed so she can focus. Twenty minutes, then take it away and offer her usual food.

Day 2

Increase slowly

If she ate some, increase the portion a little and stay on chicken. If she didn't try it at all, simply repeat day one. No escalation.

Day 3

Build familiarity

Keep increasing the fresh and reducing the kibble. Once she is settled on chicken, introduce a second recipe for variety.

Chicken first, every time — it is the easiest on the stomach and the least likely to cause a setback you then have to explain.

Speaker notes

"If she didn't try it, repeat day one" is the instruction customers most need permission for. Their instinct is to try something dramatic. Ours is to run the same gentle day again.

This is straight out of our own blog, so a customer who has read the site will hear consistency — which is itself reassuring.

E. Transition tracks

Honesty is a retention tool

"Give her a week or two" — said on day 0

On day 5 the customer thinks: "we're only halfway."

They keep going. We keep the customer.

"She'll love it" — said on day 0

On day 5 the customer thinks: "this isn't working."

They pause. And they were told to expect better.

The timeline is not a disclaimer and it is not a hedge. It is the thing that buys the food enough time to work.

Speaker notes

Some agents worry that mentioning two weeks costs them the sale. Address it head on: there is no version of this where hiding the timeline helps. It converts a cancelled subscription in week one into a cancelled subscription in week one plus a refund and a bad review.

Said with confidence, the timeline sounds like expertise. "Cats coming off dry food take a week or two, and I'll walk you through it" is a stronger sales line than "she'll love it".

F. The scorecard

The Tasting Card

A card in the top of every first box, designed to live on the fridge door. Each meal, the customer notes the recipe, whether they served it chilled or warmed, and how it went:

1 Walked away 2 Sniffed or licked 3 Ate some 4 Cleared the plate

And printed on the card itself, so nobody has to guess: "2s and 3s are progress."

A recipe that gets a 1 twice is not a problem. It is a result — and a result tells us what to swap.

Speaker notes

The four-point scale is the entire point of the card. "Did she eat it?" only has two answers and one of them is failure. Four points mean a cautious cat still generates a good day.

So: never ask "did she eat it?" Ask how she approached it. You will get a more useful answer and a calmer customer.

F. The scorecard · the script

Introducing the card in twenty seconds

Near the end of the call, after the delivery date

"One thing to look out for when the box arrives — there's a Tasting Card sitting on top of the trays. Stick it on the fridge and tick off each meal: which recipe, whether you served it chilled or warmed, and how she took it.

Takes about five seconds a meal, and it decides what goes in box 2 — we build that one around whatever she liked best.

And if a couple of recipes get a definite no, you just swap them. That's free, and there's no need to pause anything."

Speaker notes

The last line is the important one, and the instinct is to leave it out because it raises the possibility of failure. Say it anyway. The moment a customer needs to know swaps are free is the moment they are frustrated at 9pm, and that is exactly when they will not ring us — they will find the pause button.

Mentioning box 2 here also does quiet work: it makes the second delivery feel earned and personalised rather than an ambush.

F. The scorecard

Reading the card back on an inbound call

Ask, in this order
  • Which recipes has she tried?
  • Chilled or warmed — or both?
  • How many days so far?
  • Was the old food out of the way?
  • How much did you put down?
Then say what you're doing, and why

"So she's given the chicken a 2 twice, and both of those were chilled. Before we change the recipe, let's warm the next one for ten seconds — that's the single thing most likely to shift it. If she's still not interested tomorrow, I'll swap the rest of the box for the turkey and the salmon."

Speaker notes

Two things make this work. First, you are using their data, which is far more persuasive than our opinion. Second, you are naming the next step and a deadline, so the call ends with a plan rather than a hope.

Log everything they tell you. If they logged nothing digitally, this call is the only record we will ever have of what actually happened in that kitchen.

G. Swap, don't stop

Pausing is the only tool we've given them

Most people who pause in week one are not leaving us. They are trying to stop something they don't know how else to change. Give them a better tool and most of them never reach for that one.

Speaker notes

This reframe changes how the call feels. You are not defending against a cancellation, you are offering a better option to someone who currently only has one.

It also explains why the swap has to be offered before they ask. By the time they say "I want to pause", they have already decided; you are now negotiating rather than helping.

G. Swap, don't stop

The offer ladder

1
Change the texture. Chilled to warmed, or the other way. Free, instant, nothing to ship.
2
Swap the recipes. Rebuild the rest of the box, and box 2, around her best reactions.
3
Send a rescue pack. Different texture, an aroma topper and a printed plan, out next day.
4
Push the next box back a week. The normal thing to do while a cat is still settling.
5
Skip one box. Recoverable, and far better for everyone than a pause.
6
Book the transition clinic. Ten minutes with someone qualified, for fussy and kibble-fed cats.
7
Pause — deliberately, with a date. Capture the real reason and set a check-back.

Start at the top. Only move down. Nothing here costs us margin.

Speaker notes

Everyone gets this laminated. Same ladder for the CRM messages and the self-service pages, so a customer gets the same answer wherever they ask.

On rung 7: a pause with a plan and a date is recoverable. A pause at the end of an argument is not. If you genuinely get there, get there gracefully and write down exactly why.

G. Swap, don't stop

No discounts in the first 28 days

  • The data is unambiguous: price is not the objection in the first month. Food refusal is. Price only takes over after day 28.
  • A cheaper box of food the cat won't eat solves nothing. You have spent margin and the problem is still sitting in the fridge.
  • It teaches the customer that every wobble is worth a discount, and they will test that again at week 7 when the introductory price steps down anyway.
  • Fix the food instead: texture, recipe, portion, timing. That is what they actually came to you for.

Discount authority belongs to the post-28-day price conversation. Different problem, different owner, different playbook.

Speaker notes

Expect pushback, because a discount is the easiest thing to reach for under pressure and it often ends the call quickly. Acknowledge that: it ends the call, it does not save the customer.

Give them the replacement line so they are not left empty-handed: "A discount won't make her eat it. Let's fix the food."

G. Swap, don't stop

Four calls you will get this week

What they open with Your move
"She won't touch it." (Day 2, served chilled) Warm it ten seconds, clean shallow plate, kibble out of reach between meals. One change, tonight. Tell them you'll check in tomorrow.
"She ate it twice, then stopped." Usually variety fatigue rather than rejection. Rotate the recipe, keep the texture and temperature identical so we learn something.
"It made her sick." Back to chicken only, smaller portions, slower ramp. If it happens again, or there's any other symptom, straight to a vet — never talk anyone out of that.
"I want to pause." "Which ones has she tried?" Then swap the rest, rebuild box 2 around the best reactions, and offer to push the next delivery back a week.
Speaker notes

In all four: name the next step and put a time on it. "Try that tonight and I'll text you tomorrow" beats "let us know how you get on", because the second one leaves the customer alone with the decision.

On the sickness call, be unambiguous. We never discourage a vet visit, and we never diagnose. One episode during a transition is common; anything more is a vet's call, not ours.

H. Practice

Two calls, side by side

The saved call — listen for
  • The timeline stated out loud, unprompted.
  • Both textures named, with the ten seconds.
  • The swap offered before the customer asked for anything.
  • A next step with a time attached.
The lost call — listen for
  • "Cats love it."
  • "Did she eat it?"
  • Silence where the timeline should be.
  • "I can pause that for you" inside the first minute.

Both calls are real, both agents were trying to help, and only one customer is still with us.

Speaker notes

Play both clips in full, then play the lost one again and stop at the exact moment it turned — usually within the first thirty seconds, and usually a kindly-meant overpromise.

Never name the agent on the lost call, and say so before you play it. If people think clips are used to embarrass them, nobody will volunteer a call again.

H. Practice · five minutes each, then swap

Role-play briefs

A

The day-2 refusal

Kibble-fed, flagged fussy. Served chilled twice, walked away both times. Opens with "I don't think she's going to eat this — can I pause it?"

Pass: texture change before recipe change, timeline restated, swap offered, next step timed.

B

The day-4 stall

Wet-food convert. Cleared two meals, then lost interest. Customer is convinced the cat is bored and wants to cancel before box 2.

Pass: recognises variety fatigue, rotates recipe not texture, pre-notifies box 2, offers a date shift.

C

The pre-delivery wobble

Box hasn't arrived yet. Customer has been reading reviews, is now sure her cat won't eat it, and asks to cancel before it ships.

Pass: no defensiveness, plan named and sent, day-3 check-in promised, swap policy stated upfront.

Speaker notes

Brief C is the one people find hardest, because there is nothing to troubleshoot yet — no meal, no reaction, nothing to fix. The only tools are the plan, the schedule of support and the safety net. That is the point of it.

Rotate so everyone plays the customer at least once. Being the anxious owner for five minutes teaches more than any slide in this deck.

H. Practice

Assessment and certification

  • Written quiz, 85% to pass. Weighted towards the transition tracks and the texture mechanics.
  • One assessed role-play — a bowl-day-2 refusal call, scored against the retention QA scorecard. The reframe and the swap offer are both required to pass.
  • Certification is recorded against your agent ID and is required before working live first-box conversations.
  • Quarterly refresh, rebuilt each time from the previous quarter's most common refusal patterns.

This is not a hoop. Passing means you can be trusted with the conversation that decides whether a cat gets a second box.

Speaker notes

Be straight about the gating: certification controls access to first-box work. Nobody is being caught out — the quiz questions come from these slides and the role-play is the same brief they just practised.

Anyone who does not pass first time gets coaching and a retake, not a mark on their record. Say that out loud, because otherwise people assume the worst.

The three things to take to your next call

Week one is not a verdict. It's a tasting session.

1. Name both textures — chilled is jelly, ten seconds warm is gravy.
2. Say the timeline out loud, whatever it is.
3. Offer the swap before they ask for anything.

Speaker notes

Close on the three actions rather than the theory. If someone remembers nothing else from ninety minutes, these three still move the number.

Then go straight into the quiz while it is all fresh.

What you need in the room

  • One chilled tray and one warmed tray, plus plates and spoons. This is the single most persuasive prop available.
  • Printed Tasting Cards, one per person.
  • Laminated offer ladder and say-this-not-that sheet, one per desk.
  • Two call recordings: one saved, one lost. Anonymised, and say so before playing them.

Assessment quiz

Twelve questions, 85% to pass, which means no more than one wrong. Answers are for the trainer's copy only.

1. A customer's cat is currently on dry food. How long is the transition, and what do you tell them?

14 days, sometimes 3–4 weeks, and you say the number out loud on the call.

2. Give the two textures a single Marro tray can be served as, and how to get each.

Jelly-like straight from the fridge; silky gravy warmed for about ten seconds.

3. Why does a kibble-fed cat often refuse fresh food at first?

Kibble is sprayed with fat and flavour enhancers after cooking, so the cat is conditioned to crave it. Refusal is habit, not preference.

4. What does a "2" mean on the Tasting Card, and is it good news?

Sniffed or licked. Yes — 2s and 3s are progress, and the card says so.

5. A customer rings on bowl-day 2. The cat walked away from chilled chicken twice. What is your first move?

Change the texture, not the recipe. Warm it ten seconds, clean shallow plate, old food out of reach. Recipe swap only if that fails.

6. Name the first three rungs of the offer ladder, in order.

1. Change the texture. 2. Swap the recipes. 3. Send a rescue pack.

7. Why do we not offer a discount in the first 28 days?

Price is not the objection in that window — food refusal is. A cheaper box of unwanted food fixes nothing and trains the customer to expect a discount every time.

8. What are the three mechanics you teach on every first-box call?

Warm it ten seconds; clean shallow plate with old food and treats out of reach; twenty minutes then withdraw and offer the usual dinner.

9. Instead of "did she eat it?", what do you ask?

"How did she approach it — did she sniff it, lick it, or take a few bites?"

10. A customer feeds both wet and dry. Which meal do you replace first, and why?

The wet meal. It is the easy win, and a customer who has seen one win will persist through the harder dry-food week.

11. Which two things about a cat should you always capture on the call, and why?

What it is eating now and its eating type (easy, normal, fussy). They set the track, the timeline, the first texture and every message for the next fortnight.

12. Complete the sentence: "You are not selling food, you are…"

"…setting a finish line."

One-page desk aid

The four beats of the framing script

  1. Name the box — "Tuesday is her tasting box."
  2. Name the two textures — "jelly from the fridge, gravy warmed ten seconds."
  3. Name the job — "your job this week is finding what she goes for."
  4. Name the timeline — "cats off dry food take a week or two. Cautious on day one is normal."

Track lengths

  • Dry / kibble — 14 days, up to 3–4 weeks
  • Wet — 7–10 days
  • Raw — 3–7 days
  • Mixed — 10–14 days, wet meal first
  • Flagged fussy — add a week

The offer ladder

  1. Change the texture
  2. Swap the recipes
  3. Send a rescue pack
  4. Push the next box back a week
  5. Skip one box
  6. Book the transition clinic
  7. Pause — deliberately, with a date

Never

  • Promise she'll love it, or eat it straight away.
  • Ask "did she eat it?"
  • Offer a discount inside 28 days.
  • Offer a pause before you have offered a swap.
  • Talk anyone out of seeing a vet.
