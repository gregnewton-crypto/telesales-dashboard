Editorial Verdict

This deck already has the thing most training decks lack: a single, memorable idea. “We are not selling food, we are setting a finish line” is the argument, and slides 12 to 14 execute it well. The problem is that the deck loses confidence as it goes. The first half is written and proofed; the last five slides read like notes that never got tidied. An agent reaching slide 24 sees “texture trail”, “expections”, “reipice” and “cusomer” on the one slide that tells them what to actually do.

Keep The finish-line reframe, the two-finish-lines split, the “match the first serve to the last meal” table, and the three mechanics. These are the best pages in the deck.
Tighten The four recap slides (6–9) are dense and read as reference material, not training. They need a comparison table and a takeaway line each.
Rebuild Slides 12–15 (letter-spacing and a wall of script), 22, 24 and 26 (typos, broken superscripts, conflated metrics).
Add Slide numbers, a one-line takeaway strip on every content slide, and a closing “what good looks like” recap before the action points.
Five fixes, in priority order
  1. Remove the letter-spacing on slides 12, 13 and 14. Nothing else improves legibility as much for as little work.
  2. Reproof slides 22, 24 and 26. They carry 26 of the 103 corrections between them, and they are the slides agents will screenshot.
  3. Move every headline to the top-left. Seven slides currently place the title after the body content, which also scrambles the PDF export order.
  4. Break the script on slide 15 into four numbered beats. Nobody reads a four-paragraph slide out loud convincingly.
  5. Make each headline an assertion. “Day 5 is a checkpoint, not a verdict” teaches. “DAY 5+” does not.

House Style Sheet

One page to check against before the next export. Everything here is already implied by the best-written slides in the deck; it just needs applying everywhere.

Spelling UK English throughout: optimise, analysing, flavour, programme, recognise, colour, favourite. Set the document language to English (UK) so the checker catches the next one.
Numbers Words for one to nine, figures for 10 and above. Always figures for days, percentages and data (day 5, 35%, 14 days). En dashes for ranges: 7–10 days.
Headlines Sentence case, no full stop, and an assertion wherever possible. ALL CAPS only for the small kicker above the headline.
Quotes Curly quotes throughout. Double quotes for things people say; single quotes for terms being named (the ‘4% meat rule’).
The cat “They”, not “she”. In scripts, use [Cat’s name] in square brackets so agents can see the placeholder at a glance.
Boxes and days Box 1, Box 2 (capitalised). Day 1, day 5 (lower case in running text, capitalised at the start of a line). Never “Day 5+”.
Bullets Maximum six per slide, maximum 12 words each. Start with the verb. No full stops on fragments.
Temperatures “above 150°C”, “around 90°C”. No space before °C, and never a trailing plus sign.

Recap: fresh

Recap · Fresh

Real food, gently cooked, still wet

  • Real, named meat and fish — you can see the protein and the percentage
  • Gently cooked at around 90°C, then chilled or frozen, never made shelf-stable
  • High moisture, like wet food, so hydration comes with every meal
  • Complete and balanced by design, vet-approved, kitten to senior
  • Prebiotics (MOS, FOS and psyllium seed) for gut health
Takeaway strip Everything good about raw, without the risk.
07

Recap: kibble

Recap · Kibble

Cooked hard, then made tasty again on the way out

  • Extruded: mixed into a dough and forced through a machine above 150°C under pressure
  • Around 10% moisture, and 25–50% carbohydrate
  • Heat destroys nutrients such as taurine, which are then added back synthetically
  • Sprayed with fat and flavour enhancers after cooking
Takeaway strip That spray is not an accident. It is engineered to be irresistible — and cats are conditioned to want it.
08

Recap: wet

Recap · Wet

Wet is a texture, not a standard

  • Cooked above 121°C to make it shelf-stable
  • The ‘4% meat rule’: ‘with chicken’ can legally mean 4% chicken
  • Complete or complementary, a meal or a topper — always check the label
  • Many pouches use engineered flavour ‘digests’, for the same reason kibble uses sprays
Takeaway strip High moisture is a good start. It is not the whole story.

Recap: raw

Recap · Raw

Same intention as us, different risk

  • Same philosophy: real meat, high moisture, minimal processing
  • The difference is cooking and handling, not intention
  • The Food Standards Agency found harmful bacteria in 35% of the raw products it tested
Takeaway strip Fresh is raw’s philosophy, made safer and easier. That is the whole pitch.

Three myths you will hear this week

Objection handling

Three myths you will hear this week

What they sayWhat you say
“Kibble keeps her teeth clean.” Crunching shifts a little surface plaque, but does nothing below the gumline — which is where dental disease starts. The British Veterinary Association does not endorse dry food as dental care. Brushing and vet checks are the only proven routes.
“She loves her kibble, so it must suit her.” She loves the fat and flavour sprayed on after cooking. That is a conditioned response, not a nutritional verdict — the feline version of ultra-processed food.
“Premium kibble is basically the same as fresh.” Premium is genuinely better than budget: more meat, better sourcing. It is still extruded above 150°C, still around 10% moisture, and the nutrients still go back in afterwards.
Takeaway strip Never win the argument. Give them the fact and move to the plan.

Match the first serve to the last meal

First serve

Match the first serve to the last meal

Coming fromFirst serveWhy
Dry / kibble A spoonful of chicken, warmed, on a clean plate. Move old food and treats out of the way. Aroma has to do the persuading because the texture is completely new.
Wet pouches Chilled or barely warmed, mixed into the old food and increased slowly. Texture is already familiar. The palate just needs to reset after flavour digests.
Raw Straight from the fridge, mixed into the raw, increasing gradually. Both are moist and meat-rich, so most cats barely notice.
Mixed feeding Replace the wet meal first, then work on the dry one. Take the easy win first. It builds the customer’s confidence before the hard part.

19

The three mechanics that do most of the work

First serve

Three mechanics do most of the work

  1. A clean, shallow plate — not the usual bowl, not next to the kibble. Old food and treats out of reach between meals.
  2. Warm it — ten seconds in the microwave, or the tray in warm water for ten minutes. Lifts the aroma and turns jelly into gravy.
  3. Twenty minutes — then take it away and offer the usual dinner. Try again tomorrow. This is the routine, not a fallback.
Takeaway strip The most common day-one failure is not the cat. It is a full portion, served cold, next to a bowl of kibble.

How long the switch really takes

Was: “THE TRANSITION”

The transition

How long the switch really takes

Coming fromHow long, honestlyWhat you tell them
Dry / kibble 14 days, sometimes 3–4 weeks “This is the longest switch there is, and that’s normal. She’s unlearning a habit.”
Wet 7–10 days “The texture’s already familiar, so this is usually a quick one.”
Raw 3–7 days “Same sort of food, just gently cooked. Most cats barely blink.”
Mixed 10–14 days “We’ll do the wet meal first for a quick win, then the dry.”

21

Days 1 to 3

Was: “DAY 1 TO 3”, cards out of sequence

The first 72 hours

Days 1 to 3

  1. Day 1 — start small. A spoonful of warmed chicken on a clean plate, old food and treats removed. Twenty minutes, then take it away and offer the usual food.
  2. Day 2 — increase slowly. If they ate some, increase the portion a little and stay on chicken. If they did not try it, repeat day 1. No escalation.
  3. Day 3 — build familiarity. Keep increasing the fresh and reducing the kibble. Once they are settled on chicken, introduce a second recipe for variety.
Takeaway strip One variable at a time. Chicken first, everything else later.

The first 72 hours

Day 5 is a checkpoint, not a verdict

  • Day 5 is where we see the biggest drop-off

Takeaway strip Nobody should reach day 5 without knowing what day 5 is supposed to look like.

SLIDE 6 - RECAP: FRESH
Kicker: Recap - Fresh
Headline: Real food, gently cooked, still wet
- Real, named meat and fish - you can see the protein and the percentage
- Gently cooked at around 90C, then chilled or frozen, never made shelf-stable
- High moisture, like wet food, so hydration comes with every meal
- Complete and balanced by design, vet-approved, kitten to senior
- Prebiotics (MOS, FOS and psyllium seed) for gut health
Takeaway: Everything good about raw, without the risk.

SLIDE 7 - RECAP: KIBBLE
Kicker: Recap - Kibble
Headline: Cooked hard, then made tasty again on the way out
- Extruded: mixed into a dough and forced through a machine above 150C under pressure
- Around 10% moisture, and 25-50% carbohydrate
- Heat destroys nutrients such as taurine, which are then added back synthetically
- Sprayed with fat and flavour enhancers AFTER cooking
Takeaway: That spray is not an accident. It is engineered to be irresistible - and cats are conditioned to want it.

SLIDE 8 - RECAP: WET
Kicker: Recap - Wet
Headline: Wet is a texture, not a standard
- Cooked above 121C to make it shelf-stable
- The '4% meat rule': 'with chicken' can legally mean 4% chicken
- Complete or complementary, a meal or a topper - always check the label
- Many pouches use engineered flavour 'digests', for the same reason kibble uses sprays
Takeaway: High moisture is a good start. It is not the whole story.

SLIDE 9 - RECAP: RAW
Kicker: Recap - Raw
Headline: Same intention as us, different risk
- Same philosophy: real meat, high moisture, minimal processing
- The difference is cooking and handling, not intention
- The Food Standards Agency found harmful bacteria in 35% of the raw products it tested
Takeaway: Fresh is raw's philosophy, made safer and easier. That is the whole pitch.

SLIDE 10 - STATEMENT

SLIDE 11 - THREE MYTHS YOU WILL HEAR THIS WEEK
Kicker: Objection handling
Headline: Three myths you will hear this week
"Kibble keeps her teeth clean." | Crunching shifts a little surface plaque, but does nothing below the gumline - which is where dental disease starts. The British Veterinary Association does not endorse dry food as dental care. Brushing and vet checks are the only proven routes.
"She loves her kibble, so it must suit her." | She loves the fat and flavour sprayed on after cooking. That is a conditioned response, not a nutritional verdict - the feline version of ultra-processed food.
"Premium kibble is basically the same as fresh." | Premium is genuinely better than budget: more meat, better sourcing. It is still extruded above 150C, still around 10% moisture, and the nutrients still go back in afterwards.
Takeaway: Never win the argument. Give them the fact and move to the plan.

SLIDE 12 - BOX 1 IS NOT A DELIVERY
Kicker: The reframe
Headline: Box 1 is not a delivery
Stop treating it as: The first instalment of a standing order. Food arrives, cat eats it, subscription continues.
Start treating it as: A tasting and texture trial. Several recipes, two ways to serve each one, and one job: find out what this cat goes for.
Takeaway: Box 1 has one job: find the winner.

SLIDE 13 - TWO FINISH LINES, SAME CAT
Kicker: The reframe
Headline: Two finish lines, same cat
If day 5 means 'cleared the bowl': Most customers fail. Failure feels like a wasted month ahead. So they pause.
If day 5 means 'tasted it and stayed interested': Most customers pass. Passing feels like progress. So they carry on.
Takeaway: We choose which finish line the customer is running towards. We choose it on the call.

SLIDE 14 - WHAT WE NEVER PROMISE, AND WHAT WE ALWAYS PROMISE
Kicker: The reframe
Headline: What we never promise, and what we always promise
Never promise: "She'll love it." / "She'll eat it straight away." / "Cats always take to this one." / "It'll be easy."
Promise freely: A plan built around what the cat is eating right now / Support at the moments that matter / Recipe and texture swaps for the first 28 days / Box 2 built around whatever wins
Takeaway: Never promise the cat's behaviour. Always promise ours.

SLIDE 15 - WHAT THIS SOUNDS LIKE

3. The only job: "Your only job this week is to find out which flavours they love most. And a quick heads-up: if [Cat's name] is coming off dry food, a full switch can take a week or two. So if they're cautious on day 1, that's completely normal. That's exactly what the trial week is for."
4. What I've set up: "I've added a discount to your second box, I've shown you how to swap the recipes in your online account, and I've pushed your second delivery back a few days - so you've got time to test and make those changes."
Takeaway: Four beats, 30 seconds. It is the cheapest retention work we do.

Kicker: The food itself

Straight from the fridge: a cool, firm jelly.
Warmed for ten seconds: a silky gravy.

SLIDE 18 - MATCH THE FIRST SERVE TO THE LAST MEAL
Kicker: First serve
Headline: Match the first serve to the last meal
Dry / kibble | A spoonful of chicken, warmed, on a clean plate. Move old food and treats out of the way. | Aroma has to do the persuading because the texture is completely new.
Wet pouches | Chilled or barely warmed, mixed into the old food and increased slowly. | Texture is already familiar. The palate just needs to reset after flavour digests.
Raw | Straight from the fridge, mixed into the raw, increasing gradually. | Both are moist and meat-rich, so most cats barely notice.
Mixed feeding | Replace the wet meal first, then work on the dry one. | Take the easy win first. It builds the customer's confidence before the hard part.
Takeaway: Ask what they eat now. Everything else follows from the answer.

SLIDE 19 - THREE MECHANICS DO MOST OF THE WORK
Kicker: First serve
Headline: Three mechanics do most of the work
1. A clean, shallow plate - not the usual bowl, not next to the kibble. Old food and treats out of reach between meals.
2. Warm it - ten seconds in the microwave, or the tray in warm water for ten minutes. Lifts the aroma and turns jelly into gravy.
3. Twenty minutes - then take it away and offer the usual dinner. Try again tomorrow. This is the routine, not a fallback.
Takeaway: The most common day-one failure is not the cat. It is a full portion, served cold, next to a bowl of kibble.

SLIDE 20 - HOW LONG THE SWITCH REALLY TAKES
Kicker: The transition
Headline: How long the switch really takes
Dry / kibble | 14 days, sometimes 3-4 weeks | "This is the longest switch there is, and that's normal. She's unlearning a habit."
Wet | 7-10 days | "The texture's already familiar, so this is usually a quick one."
Raw | 3-7 days | "Same sort of food, just gently cooked. Most cats barely blink."
Mixed | 10-14 days | "We'll do the wet meal first for a quick win, then the dry."
Flagged fussy | Add a week to any of the above | "We plan for longer with fussy ones. Patience is genuinely the strategy."
Takeaway: Say the number out loud on the call. An expected delay is not a problem; an unexpected one is a pause.

SLIDE 21 - DAYS 1 TO 3
Kicker: The first 72 hours
Headline: Days 1 to 3
1. Day 1 - start small. A spoonful of warmed chicken on a clean plate, old food and treats removed. Twenty minutes, then take it away and offer the usual food.
2. Day 2 - increase slowly. If they ate some, increase the portion a little and stay on chicken. If they did not try it, repeat day 1. No escalation.
3. Day 3 - build familiarity. Keep increasing the fresh and reducing the kibble. Once they are settled on chicken, introduce a second recipe for variety.
Takeaway: One variable at a time. Chicken first, everything else later.

SLIDE 22 - DAY 5 IS A CHECKPOINT, NOT A VERDICT
Kicker: The first 72 hours
Headline: Day 5 is a checkpoint, not a verdict
- Day 5 is where we see the biggest drop-off
- Most boxes run on a seven-day cycle - push the second delivery back a few days so the swap has time to work
- Customers get a message two days before the box ships. Get ahead of it: tell them to log into their account and swap Box 2 to the recipes the cat liked most
- Change the mindset: a cat that has not cleared the bowl by day 5 is not a failed trial
Takeaway: Nobody should reach day 5 without knowing what day 5 is supposed to look like.

Design and Layout

Ten rules that apply to the whole deck, then the specific slides worth redrawing. The aim throughout is the same: an agent should be able to look up from their notes, read one slide in three seconds, and look back down.

1
Delete the letter-spacing Slides 12, 13 and 14 track their display type out to the point of illegibility. Wide tracking is a decorative device for three or four words at most, never for a sentence. Use size, weight and colour for emphasis instead.
2
Headline top-left, always Seven slides place the title after the body content (11, 12, 15, 16, 22, 24, 26). English readers scan from the top-left, so a headline anywhere else arrives too late — and the same ordering carries into the PDF export and any screen reader.
3
Make the headline the point, not the topic “DAY 5+” names a subject. “Day 5 is a checkpoint, not a verdict” teaches something. If someone photographs one slide, the headline alone should carry the lesson.
4
Three type sizes, no more Kicker at roughly 14pt, headline at 40–44pt, body at 20–24pt. Nothing on a training slide should ever be below 18pt, because someone will be reading it from the back of the room or on a laptop in a side window.
5
Six bullets, twelve words Six bullets per slide, twelve words each, verb first. Slides 6 and 7 currently run to five long bullets of full prose; they read as a document, not a slide.
6
Fix the colour language Use it consistently and only three ways: red for what we lose and what we never say, green for what works and what we promise, maroon and apricot for everything else. The deck already does this on slides 12 and 13 — extend it to 4, 14 and 22.
7
Add a takeaway strip One line along the bottom of every content slide, in apricot, carrying the single sentence you want remembered. It gives agents something to write down and gives you a natural place to pause.
8
Number the slides and label the sections The deck has dividers at 23 and 25 but no numbering and no section labels. Add slide numbers and a small section name in the corner, so agents can say “go back to 18” in a live session.
9
Avoid superscript ordinals The “2nd” on slide 24 breaks apart on export, leaving an orphaned “nd” below the tile. Write “second”, or use “Box 2”.
10
Design the handout, not just the deck Slides 15, 18, 20 and 21 are reference material agents will want beside them on a call. Export those four as a one-page crib sheet. Everything else can stay presentation-only.

Author Queries

Five things a copy editor cannot resolve without you. All five affect claims made to customers, so they are worth settling before the deck is rebuilt.

1. The BVA claim Slide 11 states that the British Veterinary Association does not recommend dry food for dental care. Agents will be challenged on this. We need the exact source and its wording.
2. The 35% raw figure Which Food Standards Agency study, and from when? Any statistic used against a competitor category needs a date attached.
3. Dual versus single protein Slide 16 labels the same product both ways. Confirm which recipes are dual protein, which are single protein, and the percentages to quote for each.
4. The seven-day cycle Slide 22 says most boxes are on a seven-day trial. Is that the delivery cadence, the trial period, or both? The action “push it back a couple of days” needs a specific, agreed number of days.
5. The 56.67% figure Confirm the denominator: 56.67% of all pauses, or of pauses in a given period? Slide 3 then says “23% of these”, which reads as 23% of that 57% rather than 23% of all pauses.
Suggested order of work
  • Pass one: apply the corrections log and set the document language to English (UK). Nothing structural, roughly an hour of work.
  • Pass two: drop in the revised copy and move every headline to the top-left.
  • Pass three: rebuild slides 12–15, 22, 24 and 26 to the layouts above, and add the takeaway strips.
  • Pass four: resolve the five author queries, then export the four-slide crib sheet as the agent handout.
