Three things fall out of the numbers, and the third is the one that changes what you should do first.

  1. The decay you are seeing is real and it is steep. A 1st attempt converts at 2.75 sales per 100 dials. A 5th converts at 1.14. It costs 36 dials to make a sale on attempt 1 and 88 on attempt 5.
  2. But it is driven by attempt count, not by the calendar — at least for the first month. A 30-day-old lead performs about as well as a day-old one on the same attempt number. It is only past 30 days that the floor gives way.
  3. Capping attempts is therefore not your first move. Your agents are at 44% occupancy. They already do not have enough to dial. Capping attempts today would just leave them staring at an empty queue. Supply comes first, cap discipline second — and the cap only starts paying once the list is full.
The one number you asked for. At the team's current size, order 107 fresh leads per working day — about 535 a week — and work each to a cap of 4 attempts within 30 days. That fills the phones without recycling. Going beyond that needs headcount, not leads: every additional 2.5 sales a day costs roughly one more agent and another 34 leads a day.

3Getting "fresh" defined before we count it

The model is only as good as this definition, so it is worth pinning down explicitly rather than leaving it as a feeling.

A lead is fresh while it is under both limits at once: fewer than 4 attempts placed against it, and fewer than 30 days since it arrived. Cross either line and it moves to a different economic bucket; cross both and it is dead list.

Both thresholds come from the cliffs in section 2 rather than from convention, and both are inputs in the sheet so you can move them as the data moves. The important discipline is that "how many fresh leads do we need" is meaningless unless the denominator is fixed — otherwise every conversation about lead volume quietly changes the definition to suit the answer.

One consequence worth stating plainly: a lead you buy but do not work within 30 days is worth about half what you paid for it. That is why the answer to "how many leads do we need" is never simply "as many as we can get".

4Three ways to model it

You asked to brainstorm the options before committing. Here are the three that are actually worth considering, with what each gets right and where each fails.

ACapacity-fill — "keep the dialler fed" Necessary, not sufficient
fresh leads/day  =  (agents × dials per agent-day)  ÷  dials a lead absorbs

Work out how many dials the team can place, and how many dials one lead soaks up before it is spent. Divide. This is the number that stops recycling.

Directly targets the disease you have. Simple, hard to argue with, easy to monitor daily.
Needs only data you already hold — no cost or margin assumptions.
Says nothing about whether the resulting sales number is the one the business needs.
Treats current headcount as fixed, so it can quietly enshrine an under-sized team.
BTarget-backwards — "hit the number" Right question, unsafe alone
fresh leads/day  =  sales target per day  ÷  sales per lead

Start from the commercial target and divide by what one lead is worth end-to-end. This is how most people instinctively size a lead order.

Ties lead buying to the thing the business actually cares about.
Makes the cost of a target visible before you commit to it.
Dangerous on its own. It will happily tell you to buy 272 leads a day when the team can only work 107. The surplus does not wait politely — it ages past 30 days and loses half its value. You would be paying full price for half-price leads.
Assumes sales per lead is independent of volume, which stops being true once the queue is deep enough that leads wait.
CConstrained — run both, order the lower, and flag the gap Recommended
order  =  MIN( target-driven , capacity-driven )
and if target-driven > capacity-driven, you have a hiring problem, not a lead problem

The two formulas above answer different questions, and the gap between them is itself the most useful output. Order the smaller number; report the difference as the headcount shortfall.

Cannot tell you to over-buy into an ageing queue.
Separates the two failure modes cleanly, so the conversation with finance is either "we need more leads" or "we need more agents", never a vague mixture.
Degrades gracefully — if either input is wrong, the answer is wrong in a direction you can see.
Needs both a capacity figure and a yield curve kept current. That is a monthly refresh habit, not a one-off build.

Two models I considered and rejected

  • Contact-rate targeting — "buy enough leads to hold connect rate above 40%". Tempting because connect rate is what agents feel, but it optimises the wrong thing: you can hit any connect rate you like by dialling only first attempts and throwing the rest away. Connect rate is a symptom to monitor, not a target to size against.
  • Simple ratio-of-thumb — "N leads per agent per day". This is what most teams end up with, and it is not crazy; at a 4-attempt cap it lands at roughly 43 leads per agent-day for you. But it hides the attempt cap inside the constant, so the moment anyone changes dialling policy the ratio is silently wrong and nobody notices. Fine as a sanity check, bad as the system of record.

5The recommended model, worked through

The two building blocks

Everything rests on two quantities derived from the attempt curve, both a function of where you set the cap N. Let rn be the share of leads still in play at attempt n, and vn the sales per dial on attempt n.

dials one lead absorbs
D(N)  =  r1 + r2 + … + rN

sales one lead eventually produces
S(N)  =  r1v1 + r2v2 + … + rNvN

In steady state every lead you buy is eventually worked to the cap, so daily dialling equals daily lead intake times D(N), and daily sales equals daily intake times S(N). That is the whole model. Your measured values:

6Where to set the attempt cap — and why the obvious answer is wrong

The instinct from section 2 is "attempt 5 converts at half the rate of attempt 1, so stop at 3". That instinct is right only in a world where you have leads to replace those attempts with. You do not.

The trade, stated properly

Cost per sale under a cap of N is the lead you bought plus the agent time you spent on it, divided by the sales it produced:

cost per sale  =  ( cost of a lead  +  cost of a dial × D(N) )  ÷  S(N)

Rearrange that for two adjacent caps and each extra attempt band pays for itself above a threshold lead price, expressed as a multiple of what one dial costs in agent time:

MoveExtra dials per leadExtra sales per leadWorth doing when a lead costs more thanAt €18/hr → €1.05 a dial
Cap 3 → cap 4+0.51+0.00801.7 × a dial€1.77
Cap 4 → cap 5+0.27+0.00313.5 × a dial€3.68

So if a fresh lead costs you more than about €1.77, a 4th attempt is cheaper than buying a replacement lead. Above roughly €3.68, so is a 5th. Unless your leads are very cheap, the arithmetic says work them to 4 or 5 — the opposite of the instinct.

Which regime are you in?

The cap question really reduces to one thing: which resource is scarce.

If the scarce thing is…You should maximise…Which means…
Agent hours (phones full, leads plentiful)sales per diala tight cap of 2–3, high lead volume, retire aggressively
Leads (phones idle, list empty) — you are heresales per leada loose cap of 4–5, squeeze each lead, fix supply urgently
The sequencing matters more than the cap. Today, with 44% occupancy and 60 workable leads on hand, tightening the cap would idle agents and cost you sales. Fix supply first. Once the phones are genuinely full and leads are queuing, you flip regimes and the cap becomes the right lever — at that point tighten to 3 and buy the volume instead. Build the cap into the sheet as a dial you turn, not a policy you fix once.

7Buffer, and how big the live list should be

A lead is not consumed on the day it lands. It occupies the dialler until it closes or hits the cap, so the stock you need to hold is larger than the daily flow.

Little's law: stock = arrival rate × cycle time. Your median lead takes 9 days from first dial to last (75th percentile 27 days). At 107 leads a day that is a live workable list of roughly 950–1,000 leads at any moment. If the dialler is holding far less than that, agents are working the same records repeatedly within the week, which is exactly what the 8.1-dials-per-lead figure is telling you.

On top of the working stock you want a delivery buffer, because supply is lumpy — your weekly intake has swung between 101 and 515. A reasonable rule is 3 days of untouched cover, about 320 leads, held back and not yet loaded. Less and a late delivery puts agents on dead list within a day; much more and the tail of the buffer ages past 30 days before anyone dials it.

The design principle worth keeping. The empirical part (the yield curve, measured from data) is kept strictly separate from the planning levers (target, headcount, cap, price). That is what stops the sheet rotting: when performance changes you refresh one tab, and every downstream number moves on its own. Most planning sheets die because someone hard-codes a conversion rate into the middle of a formula and nobody remembers it is there.

The refresh routine

  1. Monthly: export calls and leads from Airtable into the two paste tabs; the yield curve and list health recalculate themselves.
  2. Weekly: check the ordered-leads figure against what actually arrived. That one comparison is the whole early-warning system.
  3. Daily: the List Health verdict. If it says "empty", agents will be on dead list before lunch.

9What I would want to firm up before betting the budget on this

The direction of the answer is safe — a 41% supply ratio and 60 workable leads are not marginal findings. But several inputs deserve a second look before the numbers are used to size a purchase order.

10What I need from you to finish this

Four inputs turn this from a well-calibrated model into a purchase decision.

  1. What does a fresh lead cost, and what is the gross margin on a sale? These two numbers decide the attempt cap and tell you the most you can afford to pay per lead. Everything else in the sheet works without them; the cap recommendation does not.
  2. What is the real target? The model is currently anchored on "fill the phones", which lands at about 8 sales a day. If the business needs 20, that is a headcount conversation as much as a lead conversation, and the table in section 5 prices both.
  3. How many agents can you actually put on the phone, and for how long? The 2.5 average and 128 dials/agent-day are measured, but from a sparse sample. If the intent is 6 FTE dialling full shifts, every number in section 5 should be rebuilt on that basis.
  4. Can supply actually go to ~535 a week? Recent intake was 168. If the sources cannot deliver triple, the honest plan is to right-size the team to the leads you can get rather than keep agents at 44% occupancy — which is the more expensive of the two mistakes.
Where I would start regardless of those answers. Three things pay off before any purchase decision is made, and none of them need budget: retire anything past 30 days out of the active list so agents stop burning dials on it; tighten redial intervals so the attempt budget is spent inside the first 30 days rather than trailing to day 41; and put the List Health verdict somewhere the floor manager sees it every morning. Together those recover most of the 9.5% of dialling currently going into the dead zone.
